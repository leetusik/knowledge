The Korbit Fish Book v2: Measured Rungs, a Rest-then-Taker Exit, and a ₩5 M Live Start

How the ₩5 M Korbit book went from an assumed 10 / 12.5 / 15 / 20 % rung grid with a transfer exit to 61 measured rungs over 20 markets that sell back on the venue itself — and what the first fill looked like. Written for a novice programmer, as a sequel to The Fish Ladder. Snapshot: 2026-09-21, the day the book went live.

Background

The earlier explainer in this knowledge base, The Fish Ladder, ends with a book live on Upbit: five thin markets, rungs on both sides of the price, a Bybit perpetual short hedging each seed. That book is still running (port 8796, bin/fish.sh), and nothing in this document touches it. This document is about the second book, on a different exchange, with a different shape — and about the phase, P20, that replaced its assumed parameters with measured ones the day before it went live.

From the Upbit ladder to a Korbit bid-only book

Phase P18 screened the other Korean exchanges. Korbit (run by Mirae Asset DigitalX) came out interesting for a reason that has nothing to do with signal: its KRW markets are fee-free (0 % maker, 0 % taker on this account's plan), the minimum order is ₩5,000, the API accepts post-only and IOC orders, and there is no sandbox — the first live order is real money. Phase P19 then built a buy-only fish book on it: no seed, no ask ladder, no hedge, only bid rungs resting far below the price, at 10 / 12.5 / 15 / 20 %. The idea was the old one — a thin book, a large market sell that walks the price down through our resting bid — but the price it walks down from is not Korbit's own. It is a reference: Upbit's KRW price for the same coin, which is the liquid market. A bid 10 % under the reference is a bid 10 % under what the coin is worth elsewhere. P19's exit was to rest a sell near the reference for a day and, if it never filled, withdraw the coin to Upbit and sell it there. P19 got as far as the touch step (four real bids, cancel-and-keep proven) and stopped: the sized start and the transfer cycle were deferred to P20.

Three sentences, three changes: a new exit, a rung grid to be derived rather than chosen, and an expected return to be computed and maximised. Everything below follows from them.

A beginner's primer on the words you need (skip if you know order books and backtests)
Reference price vs venue price
The venue is Korbit, where our orders rest. The reference is what the coin is worth on a liquid market: Upbit's KRW price when Upbit lists the coin, else Bybit's USDT price converted through Korbit's own KRW-USDT rate. Every rung is priced off the reference, never off Korbit's own last trade.
Post-only, IOC, market
A post-only limit order must rest; if it would execute immediately the exchange rejects it. IOC ("immediate-or-cancel") fills what it can right now and cancels the rest. A market order names no price at all. On Korbit a market order is IOC by rule — that fact shapes the whole exit.
A wick
A one-bar excursion: the price traded far below where it opened and closed. On a dead book a modest market sell can print 30 % below the reference and be back within the hour. Our rung is in the wick's path.
Walk-forward
The honest backtest: fit the model only on weeks that come before a test block, score it on that block, slide forward, repeat. The number it produces is what the rule set would have earned on data it had never seen.
Look-ahead
Any way a "past-only" fit quietly uses the future — a market's later listing age, an event it had not yet printed. Look-ahead makes a backtest optimistic. P20 found and removed one.
Knapsack
Given items with a value and a cost and a fixed budget, pick the set worth the most. Here an item is one rung on one market, its cost is the KRW it ties up, its value is expected income per month.

Position #2 was left with 0.0046 FLR (₩0.04) — under the ₩5,000 minimum, so no further sell can be made of it, and it will close as dust at the 72-hour mark. Two of the four "never proven live" facts were settled by this one trade: the venue accepts eight decimal places of quantity, and a stage-1 sell at the reference really rests and fills.

The exit is a clock, not a price

P19's exit had a second stage that moved coin off the exchange. P20's second stage stays on Korbit and is defined by time alone. After 72 hours of resting at the reference, whatever has not sold is sold with market orders at whatever the venue pays. There is no floor. The operator's word was "unconditional", and the code carries it as a rule ("no price floor, no give-up") rather than a knob.

open →on the fill resting_sell →age ≥ rest_s (72 h) taker_selling →flat closed
open → resting_sell → ↳remainder × bid < ₩5,000 closed (dust) · taker_stuck 20 empty slices: an alarm, the loop continues

Figure 2. The position states of exit_style: rest_then_taker. Stage 1 rests one post-only sell at the reference, re-pegged every 60 seconds. Stage 2 sends market ioc sells one at a time, at least 5 seconds apart, re-sizing each from what the previous one actually filled, until nothing sellable remains.

OFF is not "pause the sale". Turning the book off cancels every resting order and keeps every position — nothing is sold at a discount on an operator pause. But the 72-hour clock is the position's age, measured from the moment it opened and restored from the ledger on a restart. A position that is already overdue when the book comes back is sold on the first eligible tick. The runbook puts it in bold: the clock keeps running while the book is off, down, or crashed.

Why a loop and not one order? Because of two facts S1 read in the venue's documentation, both of which P19 had recorded the other way round. Korbit's price protection (pp) is opt-in, not a default 5 % cap — so an unprotected market sell really will walk a dead book. And a Korbit market order is IOC-only: whatever the book cannot fill right now is cancelled, not rested. So "sell it all, unconditionally" cannot be one request; it is send, read filledQty, send the remainder, until flat. That is what taker_selling does.

Why a 5 % rung does not earn 5 %

Buying at ref × 0.95 and selling at ref is a 5.26 % gain — if the reference stands still. It never does. P20 measured the exit per event from the hourly panel: for every wick that reached a depth, did the venue's high get back to the reference within 72 hours (sale at the reference then), or not (sale at the venue's close at 72 hours, less the measured taker haircut and a stress margin)? research/p20/decision.md, the pooled exit table. "At g = 5 % the arithmetic capture of buying at ref × 0.95 and selling at ref would be 5.26 %, and the measured expectation is 2.81 % — because the reference falls during the hold of exactly the events that fill a rung."

Key idea: deeper is safer per fill, not just richer. A rung 50 % under the reference is hit only by a wick, and wicks revert: the position is sold at a loss 3.8 % of the time, against 8.6 % at 5 %. What kills the deep rungs is not risk but rate: 26 events in a year across 165 markets at 50 %, five at 75 %. The operator's "if the book is clean I can order the 50 % fish" was tested and answered by S1 — a −50 % bid does not catch the same flash sell as a −20 % one; only one wick in ten keeps going. The strategy survived the test because the deep rung earns 2.5× per fill, and the optimiser is what weighs the two.

One more thing the table says. Patience beats price: in the (m, T) grid, the margin m is monotonically harmful and T dominates. Resting at ref × 0.98 instead of at the reference gives away 26 % of the income; cutting the hold to 24 hours costs only 3.2 %; cutting it to one hour makes every depth below 20 % negative, because "a position that has not recovered in an hour is sold into the same hole that filled it".

The optimiser: a knapsack walked in rank order

Now the second sentence of the sketch — is the grid statistical or intuition? — becomes a concrete procedure. Every (market, depth) pair is an item. Its value per month is rate × Q × E[ret]: how often that depth is touched per month, times the KRW resting there, times the expected return per fill from the table above. Its cost is Q plus a small reserve for the positions it will hold. The key it is ranked by is rate × E[ret], income per won, which does not depend on Q — so walking items in key order and stopping when the budget runs out is the fractional-knapsack optimum. Five rules trim the candidate set first, and each is a measurement, not a taste:

  • ₩250,000 per rung and Q ≤ the 25th percentile of the KRW that actually traded through that depth on the 1-minute panel — a rung cannot be bigger than the wicks that fill it.
  • ₩1,000,000 per market (it bound on exactly one market, KRW-SPACE, and trimmed HYPE from ₩1.12 M).
  • At least 60 days of reference history — three markets with 10 to 30 days were dropped; they re-enter by themselves at the next regeneration once their history crosses the line.
  • No rung more than one grid step past the deepest depth the market has ever printed a transient event at, and none where the market's own shape test reads below.
  • A reference that is the wrong coin excludes the market — KRW-VELO on Korbit is Velodrome; VELOUSDT on Bybit is Velo Protocol; the symbol collision priced it 361 % wrong.

The exit's price, which the phase was opened to worry about, barely moves the answer, because the taker leg is reached on only about 8 % of fills at a 72-hour hold.

Two panels, and the look-ahead that was worth ₩62,800 a month

S1's biggest find was accidental. Korbit's candle endpoint keeps a fixed count of intervals — about 50,000 — so a 1-minute pull reaches 34.7 days back and an hourly pull reaches a year. That gave P20 two panels: panel A, one minute, 34 days, 531 events; panel B, hourly, 365 days, 4,157 events. At 50 % depth panel A holds three events in total across every market; panel B holds 26. So rates and recovery probabilities are fitted on the year, and capacity — how much KRW trades through a level in the minute that touches it — comes from the minute panel only. A control run showed the two panels differ by epoch (1.16–1.94×, one-signed), not by resolution.

Figure 4. F1's correction. S3's walk-forward re-fitted the rates per fold but took the rule inputs — a market's listing age, the deepest depth it had printed, its shape verdict — from the full year, so a fold knew things about a market's future. Re-deriving them per fold moved the median from ₩510,842 to ₩448,042 (−12.29 %); the extrapolation limit carried most of it, and the in-sample configuration did not change by a byte.

Both readings of the model are optimistic in a stated direction: the census treats a touch as a fill, and at 5 % the queue ahead of a rung is thickest (a median ₩1.44 M resting above it). That is why the first live month's job is to report realised-versus-modelled fills per rung before any lever — the ₩500 K per-rung cap, +11 % — is pulled.

2. Per-market rungs, per-rung sizes

The fish-ladder book had one rungs_pct list and one order_krw for every market. P20 lets both be maps, and everything that reads them goes through accessors — the code's own docstring warns that a ladder may be gapped:

def rungs_pct_for(self, market: str) -> list[float]:
    """The rungs armed on **one** market (D13): its list, or the default.

    Ascending and distinct, and possibly **gapped** — ``KRW-FLR`` is
    5 / 7.5 / 10 + 30 / 40 / 50 / 60 — so a caller may index into this list,
    but must never infer the next depth from a grid.
    """
    if isinstance(self.rungs_pct, dict):
        return [float(g) for g in self.rungs_pct.get(
            market, self.rungs_pct[RUNGS_PCT_DEFAULT_KEY])]
    return [float(g) for g in self.rungs_pct]

Quiz

Five questions. Click an answer for immediate feedback.

1. A rung 5 % under the reference fills and the position later sells at the reference. Buying at ref × 0.95 and selling at ref is +5.26 %. Why does the model expect only 2.81 % per fill at this depth?

The exit is measured per event in KRW terms — sale ÷ entry − 1 — so the coin's own drift over the hold is inside the return by construction. At 5 % that drift costs almost half the arithmetic capture; at 50 % it costs almost nothing.

2. A position has been resting its sell for 70 hours when the operator runs go_live.sh --off. Ten hours later they turn the book back on. What happens to that position?

The timeout is one comparison in step: pos.age_s(ts) >= rest_s, with age measured from opened_ts as restored from the ledger. The runbook: "OFF is not 'pause the sale'".

3. The operator asked for an unconditional market sell at the timeout. Why does the code implement it as a loop of market ioc orders re-sized from filledQty, rather than one request?

S1 read both facts from the venue's documentation — and both corrected P19's notes, which had recorded a default 5 % protection cap. A trimmed or cancelled order still closes status: "filled", which is why the loop reads sizes, never status words.

4. Slice S3 reported the walk-forward median at ₩510,842 a month; fix F1 re-stated it at ₩448,042, and the phase adopted the lower number. What changed between the two?

The rates were already fold-local; the rules were not, so a fold knew a market's later listing age and later deep events. Removing that look-ahead was worth ₩62,800 a month, mostly through the extrapolation limit, and changed nothing in sample.

5. The first go-live attempt at 09:23 was refused at ON with eight markets reported missing, though all eight were trading normally on Korbit. Why, and what fixed it?

The preflight's reference row had passed throughout, because it asks the feed rather than a listing. The 09:56 attempt's ON row read missing: [] and missing_korbit: [], and the log carried no market_missing at all.
