Six Documents to a Hundred: The P52 Content-Agent Curation Gate

A walkthrough of changple5 phase P52 — how an AI writing agent went from drafting an article out of six source posts to a hundred, why the fix was three deleted bounds rather than a bigger search, and how a read-only approval screen became the place a human actually steers the work.

Background — the machine before the change

If you are new to this codebase

changple5 runs an internal tool that Changple staff use to write library articles. An operator types a short brief — say, write about the 당술샘 cafe — and a content agent (a program that drives a large language model, or LLM, through several steps) does the rest: it searches an indexed archive of Naver-Cafe posts, drafts an article grounded in what it found, and hands it back for review and publishing.

Two words you need before anything else:

  • Retrieval — the search step. The archive is searched two ways at once, by keyword (lexical) and by meaning (vector, using numeric embeddings of the text), the two result lists are fused, and a reranker model reorders the survivors by how relevant they actually look. What comes back is a ranked list of candidate posts.
  • 구성안 (gu-seong-an, "composition plan") — the article's outline. The agent proposes one, a human approves it, and only then does the agent write the body. That approval moment is the system's gate.

Retrieval is not the content agent's private property. The very same module serves the consumer chat product, the consulting report generator, an author-posts feature, and a public HTTP endpoint that outside integrations call. One search implementation, five consumers. Hold on to that fact — it is the hinge of the whole phase.

The symptom the operator reported

Articles were being written from roughly six source posts. Six is not enough to write a substantial grounded article, and the operator knew the archive held far more than six posts about the subjects being requested.

The obvious diagnosis — "the search is too narrow, widen it" — was wrong, and the engineering interest of this phase starts right there.

The actual cause: three compounding truncations

The search itself was returning plenty. Three separate bounds downstream were throwing the results away:

  1. CANDIDATES_PER_QUERY = 6 — every single search was sliced to its first six hits before anything else looked at it.
  2. MAX_RESEARCH_DOCUMENTS = 24 — a cap on the whole collected corpus.
  3. Worst of the three: that cap was enforced as a break in the middle of a candidate list. So when the budget ran out halfway through search #4's results, search #4 was chopped arbitrarily — not deprioritised by relevance, just cut wherever the loop happened to be standing.

Measured on a live development stack, replaying the old boundary over five plausible search queries, the pool went 6 → 9 → 9 → 9 → 9. It plateaued. Running more searches could not help, because the loss was not in the searching.

There was a second, unrelated complaint bundled into the same phase: at the approval screen, photo thumbnails did not render in production. And a third, larger one — the operator could see the proposed 구성안 but could not change it. Approval was all-or-nothing; the only way to reshape an outline was to write feedback in a chat thread and wait for a whole new proposal.

Intuition — three ideas that carry the whole phase

Idea 1 — Widen by call site, not by default

The content agent needs about a hundred candidate posts. A chat turn needs about sixteen: a chat user is waiting, and every extra candidate costs latency and reranker tokens. One shared module, two legitimately different appetites.

The tempting move is to raise the shared numbers. It is one line and it works. It also silently re-prices every other consumer — chat, consulting, author posts, the public endpoint — for the benefit of one.

The move P52 made instead: the shared function already accepted its widths as optional keyword arguments with defaults. So the content agent passes its own widths, from its own module, and the shared file is not edited at all.

What makes this more than a stylistic preference is the proof it enables. "We changed the shared module carefully" is an argument. "git diff on the shared package is empty" is a fact — and it was, across all eleven slices of work. The phase then nailed the fact down with a test that asserts the six shared constants still equal their original values, with the operator's own instruction quoted in the test's docstring. Anyone who later "just bumps a default" gets a red test with an explanation attached.

A quiet bonus: the cache came along for free

Search results are cached, and the cache key already included the four width values. So the wide content-agent call lands on its own cache entry and cannot poison the narrow chat one. Nobody designed that for this change; it fell out of a key that was honest about its inputs. Good cache keys pay rent years later.

Idea 2 — Trim once, at the end, by rank

The old code enforced its budget during collection, so where the budget ran out decided which documents survived. The new code collects everything, remembers how each post scored, and trims once at the end by relevance.

For this to work at all, the ranking signal has to survive the journey. Previously the reranker's ordering was computed and then thrown away at the content agent's boundary — nothing downstream could sort or trim by relevance even in principle. Now each collected post carries its best rank across every query that surfaced it (a duplicate hit upgrades a post rather than being ignored) plus its fused score.

Same five queries, new loop: 24 → 37 → 69 → 82 → 110, trimmed to 100. Six to a hundred, with no change to how the searching works.

Idea 3 — A hundred documents is a human problem, not a machine one

Here the phase turns a corner. A hundred candidates is worse than six unless something judges them. Two things now do.

First, the machine judges relevance: an LLM filter keeps only posts with dedicated content about the subject (not passing mentions), and a second LLM pass sorts the survivors into categories it invents per subject — 브랜드 기획 및 창업, 메뉴 구성 및 주방 효율화 — with a post allowed to belong to more than one.

Second, and this is the part that changes the product, the human judges editorially. The approval screen stopped being a receipt and became a workbench: rename a category, reorder them, search the archive and add a document, drop one, assign photos, empty a category to delete it. Then one button.

A hard rule governed that gate: one gate, not two. It was tempting to add a "review the filtered documents" stop before the outline. The phase forbade it, because a future automatic mode has to be able to drive this pipeline unattended, and every extra mandatory human stop is a wall that mode will hit. Everything folded into the approval moment that already existed.

Where the honest ceiling turned out to be

Measured on a development corpus for the subject 당술샘: 21 archive rows mention it, but 8 were never ingested — no embedding, therefore invisible to search, including to the consumer chat product. Of the 13 reachable, 10 made the pool and the filter kept 9. Widening the search further returned the same 10. So the remaining ceiling is ingestion coverage, not retrieval width and not the filter — a genuinely useful thing to learn, and now its own tracked work item.

Code — walking the real diff

Eleven slices, about 11,600 inserted lines across the Python agent, the Django backend and the Next.js frontend. We will walk the five places where the interesting decisions live.

1. The wrapper that widens nothing but itself

The entire "wide retrieval" feature is one new 84-line module, apps/agent/app/content_drafts/retrieval.py:

CONTENT_LEXICAL_CANDIDATE_LIMIT = 64      # shared default: 32
CONTENT_VECTOR_CANDIDATE_LIMIT = 64       # shared default: 32
CONTENT_VECTOR_PER_VARIANT_LIMIT = 24     # shared default: 12
CONTENT_RERANK_CANDIDATE_LIMIT = 40       # shared default: 16

def search_corpus_candidates(query, *, settings):
    result = build_ranked_retrieval_result(      # the SHARED entry point
        query,
        settings=settings,
        lexical_limit=CONTENT_LEXICAL_CANDIDATE_LIMIT,
        vector_limit=CONTENT_VECTOR_CANDIDATE_LIMIT,
        vector_per_variant_limit=CONTENT_VECTOR_PER_VARIANT_LIMIT,
        rerank_limit=CONTENT_RERANK_CANDIDATE_LIMIT,
    )
    return tuple(
        ScoredCorpusCandidate(
            metadata=reranked.candidate.metadata,
            rank=int(reranked.rerank_rank),                       # <- kept, not discarded
            score=float(getattr(reranked.candidate, "fusion_score", 0.0) or 0.0),
        )
        for reranked in result.reranked_candidates
    )

Two things worth noticing. The function calls the shared entry point unchanged — the "new retrieval path" is a call-site convention, not new machinery. And the return type keeps rank and score, the two signals the old boundary dropped; that is what makes trim-by-relevance possible later.

The claim that nobody else widened is checkable in one command:

$ grep -rn "lexical_limit=\|vector_limit=\|vector_per_variant_limit=\|rerank_limit=" \
       apps/agent/app/ | grep -v "^apps/agent/app/retrieval/"

apps/agent/app/content_drafts/retrieval.py:73:        vector_limit=...
apps/agent/app/content_drafts/retrieval.py:74:        vector_per_variant_limit=...

Four hits, one file. Everyone else takes the defaults, which is to say: everyone else behaves exactly as they did before.

The tripwire

A convention that only lives in reviewers' heads decays. This one is pinned by a test whose docstring is an argument, not a description:

def test_shared_retrieval_widths_are_frozen() -> None:
    """P52 regression tripwire — DO NOT relax to make a caller wider.
    ... A caller that needs a wider search passes per-call keyword
    overrides from its own module, the way content_drafts/retrieval.py
    does; it never edits these values."""
    assert DEFAULT_RERANK_CANDIDATE_LIMIT == 16
    assert DEFAULT_LEXICAL_CANDIDATE_LIMIT == 32
    ...

A test that fails loudly and then tells you what to do instead is worth several paragraphs of documentation nobody reads.

2. Two LLM stages that are not allowed to fail

content_drafts/curation.py adds the relevance filter and the categorizer. The design pressure here is different from the retrieval work: these stages run on every outline turn, and a model that returns nonsense must not take the job down with it.

Three defences, in order of how much they matter:

  1. Structured output. Both stages use the LLM provider's schema-constrained mode at temperature 0, so the model returns an id list by construction rather than prose you have to parse. Notably this is the content agent's only use of it — everything else goes through a hand-rolled JSON extractor — and the exception is argued in the module's own docstring rather than left as a silent inconsistency.
  2. Fallbacks at every failure point. Pool of 8 or fewer? Skip the model entirely. Fewer than 8 survivors? Take the pool's top 8 by rank. Exception? Keep the whole pool. Categorizer returns nothing usable? One category named 전체 holding the top 10. Every path logs a warning and continues.
  3. Caps are code, never the model. The prompt asks for at most 10 categories of at most 10 posts, but the prompt is not the enforcement. Ids outside the pool are dropped, duplicates collapsed, the lists truncated, empty categories removed — all in Python, after the model has spoken.

A small decision with a long shadow

Each category gets a stable key, minted server-side as cat-1 … cat-10 after ordering. Why not derive it from the display name? Because the human is about to rename these categories, and a rename must not change identity — later code maps an approved category back to its section by key. Identity and label are different things; conflating them is one of the most common ways an editable UI corrupts its own data.

3. The gate as a three-party contract

The outline payload is now written by the agent, validated and re-derived by the backend, and edited by the browser. Three parties, one shape — which is exactly the situation where things quietly rot unless somebody writes the rules down. Here they are, as the backend enforces them (content/draft_serializers.py, content/draft_views.py):

RuleWhy
No body, or {} → old behaviour exactlyThe un-edited path must stay byte-for-byte what it was. Backward compatibility is not a courtesy here; older outlines are still live in the database.
An edit becomes a new outline row at number + 1, the proposal marked supersededNever mutate in place. The agent's proposal stays readable, the lineage is a history you can walk, and revert is possible without resurrecting rows.
Unknown category key → 400; omitted key → server mints oneThe client never chooses identity.
Caps checked in the serializer, before any database workCheap rejection; a 400 costs no transaction.
A category with no documents is dropped, not rejected, and named back in dropped_categoriesEmptying a category is how a human says "delete this". Rejecting it would make the natural gesture an error. Naming it back makes the consequence visible.
A post id outside the pool must resolve against the document table, or 400The human can add any archive document by keyword search; the writer must actually receive it. This bridge is load-bearing — without it "search and add" would appear to work and silently do nothing.

And then the rule that is a genuine trap:

if image_keys_supplied:
    # The edited 구성안 is authoritative on images too: a candidate the
    # operator left out of every category is excluded.
    included = {k for category in categories for k in category["image_keys"]}
    payload["image_candidates"] = [
        {**entry, "included": str(entry.get("key", "")) in included}
        for entry in (payload.get("image_candidates") or []) ...
    ]

Read that as a client author. "Photos are included if some category claims them" — so a client that edits only the article's text, and dutifully sends every field, sends image_keys: [] everywhere and deletes every photo from the article. The backend defends the obvious half (if no category mentions images at all, leave everything alone). The frontend has to defend the rest, and does: on load it seeds each category's photo assignment by reading the existing image plan, so a text-only edit reproduces today's photo set exactly.

The general shape of this bug

Any API where absence means removal hands every client the same footgun. If you cannot avoid the semantics, do what this one did: make total absence mean "no opinion", make partial presence mean "authoritative", and write the seeding rule down where the client author will hit it. Both halves of that are in the shipped documentation now.

4. The frontend: a buffer with no save button

The editing surface (four new React modules, plus lib/miracle-agent-outline-buffer.ts) makes one strong architectural choice: the edit buffer is a pure module — a shape, a reducer, counters, validation, the local estimate, the request builder, persistence. No React, no DOM, no network; the caller passes in the clock and a storage-shaped object. Every trap therefore lives somewhere unit-testable instead of being tangled in a component.

Two product decisions worth stealing:

  • No save button, and no save endpoint. Edits live in the browser until the single approve. There is exactly one write, which means there is exactly one thing that can go wrong, and no half-saved server state to reconcile.
  • The 2.5-second poll never overwrites your work — it offers. If a new outline arrives while you have edits, the screen keeps yours and shows three explicit choices (keep editing / preview the new one / switch to it) plus a "auto-refresh paused" chip. With no edits it simply adopts the new one and says so.

There is even a deliberate disagreement in the UI: the browser's length estimate counts only non-empty categories (the empty ones are about to be dropped), while the server's counts all of them, so the two differ until you approve and then match. Rather than paper over it, the design names the excluded category in a tooltip and the documentation says in as many words: this is not a bug, do not "fix" it.

5. Two small fixes with a lesson each

The photo previews were broken two independent ways, and the diagnosis is more instructive than the patch.

  • Attachment images pointed at /media/..., a path nothing routed. The reverse proxy defines /healthz, /agent-api/, /api/ and / — so /media/… fell through to the frontend app and 404'd. The fix was not to add media routing but to notice that the rest of the product already serves images through an API route, and bring this path in line with it.
  • Thumbnails hotlinked a CDN that blocks hotlinking. A code comment claimed the practice was "the repo norm — proven live". It was not: the thing it cited turned out to be served through the backend all along, and a live check showed the CDN returns 200 with no referrer and 403 with our origin's referrer — precisely what a browser sends. The fix is a job-scoped proxy: the requested key must already exist in that job's own outline (so it is not an open image proxy), and the fetch goes through the existing allowlisted downloader.

The comment was the bug

A confident comment asserting a precedent, where the precedent did not exist, is why this shipped broken and stayed broken: an onError placeholder made it degrade gracefully into invisibility. When a comment justifies a risky choice by citing prior art, the citation is worth thirty seconds of checking.

How the phase ended

Honestly, and this matters for reading the code. Eleven slices shipped; the twelfth — the only planned live end-to-end run — was never executed. The operator closed the phase early to rebuild the agent on a different runtime, and the validation moved intact to a tracked deferred job. So every claim above rests on unit tests, static screenshots, synthetic jobs and targeted live checks of individual pieces. The whole path from collect to publish has not run once end to end. The phase review said so in the shipped documentation rather than letting a green test suite imply otherwise.

Best practices & next steps

Where this aligns with prevailing practice

The gate is a textbook propose-then-commit approval pattern. The industry framing is that the agent never executes a high-impact action directly — it proposes, and a human commits — with irreversible or high-stakes steps behind a hard gate while cheap reversible ones flow through (StackAI's write-up on HITL approval workflows — stackai.com). P52 sits exactly there: search and filtering run unattended, publishing an article does not.

"One gate, not two" is the mainstream warning, not a local quirk. The standard guidance is to minimise interruptions — too many gates and users abandon the agent — and to place gates at genuine decision points rather than sprinkling prompts (Permit.io's HITL best-practices guide — permit.io). The phase's refusal to add a second, pre-outline review stop is that advice applied under its own name.

Pausing a pipeline requires durable state, and this one has it. The published pattern is a "state-managed interruption": the agent's context is persistently checkpointed and resumed by an external trigger, and what you surface to the reviewer is the pending action plus enough context to judge it (Machine Learning Mastery's approval-gate walkthrough — machinelearningmastery.com). Here the durable state is the stored outline row with its research state, and the reviewer context is the category cards with their documents and photos.

Keeping every proposal as a new superseding row is the audit trail those guides ask for — "log everything: every request, approval and denial" (permit.io, above). An edited approval writes a new numbered outline, marks the old one superseded, and posts a server-composed summary line to the thread. The human's decision is recoverable after the fact, which is the entire point.

Per-consumer retrieval configuration over a shared implementation is also the prevailing shape. Guidance on running one retrieval stack for many consumers is to keep a single physical collection and vary retriever configuration per caller rather than forking the data layout or the module (LlamaIndex's hybrid multi-tenant Qdrant guide — llamaindex.ai), with per-consumer settings applied at each pipeline stage (Mavik Labs on multi-tenant RAG — maviklabs.com). P52's call-site widths are the minimal version of that.

Where it deliberately diverges

No policy engine, no risk classifier. The literature recommends routing actions through a policy layer instead of hard-coded conditionals, so approval rules stay declarative and versioned (permit.io, above). P52 hard-codes exactly one gate in the job status machine. For a single-operator internal tool with one irreversible action, a policy engine would be ceremony; the divergence is a scale judgment, and it stops being right the moment a second reviewer role appears.

The edit buffer is single-browser by design. Edits live in browser storage until approval — no server-side draft state, no locking, no multi-seat story. That is a deliberate simplification for a one-operator admin surface (the restore banner says so out loud), and the phase recorded that multi-seat use would be a contract change rather than a tweak.

Two image routes are unauthenticated. An <img> tag cannot carry a bearer token, so both routes are public with an unguessable UUID as the credential — matching the pattern already used for published media, but consciously widening an otherwise admin-only surface. The phase wrote the residual risk into the security documentation rather than leaving it implied: an unpublished draft's image is fetchable by anyone holding the id, and neither route is rate-limited.

Concrete next steps

  1. Run the thing end to end, once. The pipeline has never executed as a whole. Everything above is well-tested in parts and unproven as a system — that gap is the top priority regardless of which agent runtime the rebuild lands on.
  2. Fix the ingestion coverage, not the retrieval. Documents that were never ingested are invisible to every search consumer, chat included. That is a bigger and quieter problem than the one this phase set out to solve, and the phase's own measurements found it.
  3. Close the environment-variable parity gap. The new model role's env variable is not wired into either compose file, so setting it in production is a silent no-op. Harmless today because a database store overrides it — exactly the kind of harmless that is unpleasant to debug in a year.
  4. Consider a risk-tiered gate before adding an automatic mode. If unattended generation ever ships, the single hard gate becomes the thing that blocks it. The three-tier model (auto-approve / notify-and-undo / hard block) is the standard way through, and deciding which tier "publish an article" belongs to is a product question worth asking early.

Quiz

1. The content agent was drafting from only ~6 documents. What was the actual root cause?

Replaying the old boundary over five queries plateaued at 6 → 9 → 9 → 9 → 9: more searching could not help, because the loss happened after searching. Removing the three bounds alone took the same five queries to 110.

2. Why express the wider search as per-call keyword arguments instead of raising the shared module's defaults?

Chat, consulting, author posts and a public endpoint all call the same function. The unedited shared package — provable with one git diff — is the whole point, and a frozen-constants test keeps it that way.

3. A client sends an edited outline where every category carries image_keys: []. What happens, and why does it matter?

Absence-means-removal semantics. The backend defends only the total-silence case (no category mentions images at all → leave everything alone); a client that "dutifully sends every field" walks straight into it unless it seeds first.

4. Category keys are minted server-side as cat-1 … cat-N after ordering, rather than derived from the category's display name. Why?

Identity and label are different things. Deriving the key from the name would make every rename a delete-plus-create, silently breaking the mapping from category to written section.

5. The browser's length estimate and the server's disagree while you are editing, then match after approval. What is the correct reaction?

Both numbers are correct about different moments — "what approving would produce" versus "what is currently stored". The design surfaces the reason rather than papering over it, and the documentation explicitly says not to "fix" it.
