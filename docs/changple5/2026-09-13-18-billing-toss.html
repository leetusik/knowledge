Billing: the Toss subscription lifecycle from checkout to reconciliation

Explainer #18 of the changple5 curriculum. Everything else in this codebase can be retried, re-indexed or regenerated. apps/backend/billing/ is the one subsystem where a bug costs somebody real money — and it was written with that in mind. This page walks the subscription lifecycle end to end and, more importantly, the five mechanisms that keep it correct when the process dies, the network hangs, a webhook is lost, or the same sweep runs twice.

Background

What is actually being sold

One product, one price, one tier. A Plan row (billing/models.py:33) holds a stable machine code ("pro"), a display name, and price_krw — a plain integer number of Korean won. The app bills whatever row services.get_active_plan() returns:

# apps/backend/billing/services.py:211
def get_active_plan() -> Plan | None:
    """The plan the checkout flow bills. ``None`` ⇒ billing is treated as dormant
    (checkout/confirm answer 503, status reports ``enabled=false``)."""

    return Plan.objects.filter(is_active=True).order_by("id").first()

Price is data, not code: the operator edits it in Django admin, and a migration history records the moves (0002_seed_pro_plan at 19,000 KRW/월, 0005_pro_price_29000 at today's 29,000 KRW/월). Buying it flips exactly one column — User.tier from free to pro — and stores exactly one card, as a Toss billing key.

Jargon, once: a billing key (빌링키) is a durable token Toss gives a merchant in exchange for a customer's one-time card authorisation. It stands in for the card: you never see or store the number, you charge the key. A customerKey is the merchant's own id for that customer, echoed back on every call. And idempotency means "doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once" — the single most important word on this page.

The one Toss fact everything else follows from

Here is the sentence at the top of billing/renewals.py, and it is the design premise of half this subsystem:

A Toss billing key is charge-on-demand — Toss does **not** auto-schedule
renewals (DECOMP finding), so the merchant owns the cadence.

Many payment providers (Stripe's Subscriptions, for instance) will run the recurring schedule for you: you describe a plan, they bill it monthly and tell you what happened. Toss's billing-key API does not. It gives you one verb — "charge this key for this amount, now" — and nothing else. Every monthly renewal in this product exists because our Celery beat woke up at 02:00 UTC and decided it was due. That single fact is why renewals.py (558 lines) is the largest module in the app and why the dunning ladder, the retry timers and the reconciliation sweep are all ours to get right.

Dormant by default

Billing ships switched off. config/settings.py (lines 201–209) reads three values, and the first one is the master switch:

# apps/backend/config/settings.py

# an unset TOSS_SECRET_KEY makes the whole billing surface answer 503 with no
# network call (billing/toss.py:_require_configured) and the status endpoint

Every service entry point calls toss._require_configured() as its literal first statement, so an unconfigured deployment answers 503 before it takes a lock, writes a row or opens a socket. GET /api/billing/status reports {"enabled": false, "plan": {...}} — the plan payload rides along even while disabled so the frontend can still render the pricing copy behind a "결제 준비 중" state.

Three operational facts live in docs/current/operations.md §"Toss Billing (P27)" and are worth knowing but not re-teaching here:

  • Turning billing on is a key deployment, never a code change — TOSS_SECRET_KEY on django_backend + celery_worker + celery_beat (all three: the sweeps charge from the workers), and NEXT_PUBLIC_TOSS_CLIENT_KEY as a web build arg.
  • The webhook URL registered in the Toss 개발자센터 is https://miracle.changple.ai/api/billing/webhook. A successful billing charge sends no webhook at all, so the registration is defensive; the reconciliation sweep is what actually protects the money.
  • The go-live history — sandbox test_ keys ran on production during the card 심사 window, and swapping to live_ keys requires administratively expiring every sandbox-era subscription in the same window (a test_ key cannot be charged with a live_ secret). That is a written runbook: docs/reference/toss-live-cutover-runbook.md.

Two partial unique constraints do real work. A partial unique index is one that applies only to rows matching a condition — the database enforces a rule on part of the table. Both of billing's are load-bearing:

Read the first one carefully: the excluded status is expired — so past_due counts as live. That is why a past-due user gets a 409 already_subscribed from /checkout and cannot "fix" a failing card by re-subscribing. The only exits from past_due are a successful dunning retry (→ active) or expiry (→ free, after which a fresh checkout is allowed) — or the card-change endpoint pair, which exists precisely because of this.

The lifecycle, as a state machine

Intuition

Ask one question of this subsystem and everything else falls out of the answer:

What happens if the process dies in the instant right after Toss says DONE, but before our database commits?

The customer's card has been charged. Our database knows nothing about it. Nobody is going to tell us — a successful billing charge sends no webhook.

A naive implementation retries the charge and bills the customer twice. A slightly less naive one gives up and silently keeps money for a subscription it never activated. The interesting engineering is entirely in the third option: make the retry provably be the same request, and then make something come back later to check. Five mechanisms carry that weight.

The five safety mechanisms

1 · One lock, one order

Every billing mutation for a user takes select_for_update() on that user's accounts_user row first, then touches BillingKey / Subscription / Payment under it. One lock, always in the same order, so two concurrent money paths for one user cannot interleave — and deadlock between two users is structurally impossible.

2 · A deterministic order id that is the idempotency key

A renewal's order_id is computed, not generated: sub-42-20261001-a1. It is sent to Toss as both the orderId and the Idempotency-Key header. Recompute it tomorrow and you get the same string, so Toss replays the original outcome instead of charging again.

3 · failure_code = "" — the outcome-unknown marker

A declined charge stores Toss's real code. A charge whose outcome we never learned (timeout, 5xx, "still processing") stores an empty code. One blank string is the entire interface between "the money path gave up" and "the backstop must look into it".

4 · A daily reconciliation sweep anchored on Payment rows

At 02:30 UTC every failed row with a blank code from the last 7 days is re-queried against Toss by our own order id and resolved. Because it scans our rows rather than an event feed, it works even if every webhook we ever get is lost.

5 · Webhooks are hints, never truth

The receiver is unauthenticated, answers 200 to everything, and mutates nothing from the payload. It re-queries Toss authoritatively and only then runs the same repair function the sweep uses. A webhook can therefore only make the system faster, never wrong.

+ Amounts come from the database

A quiet sixth: the charged amount is always plan.price_krw read server-side. No client-supplied amount is accepted anywhere, at any endpoint, ever.

What catches what

Here is the same idea as a table of disasters. Read the right-hand column as the answer to "who notices?".

What goes wrongState just afterWhat catches it
Crash after Toss says DONE, before our commit (renewal) Nothing written at all — the transaction rolled back. Toss has the money. Mechanism 2. renewal_attempt_count and current_period_end never moved, so tomorrow's sweep computes the same order_id, sends the same Idempotency-Key, and Toss replays the original success. The period advances a day late; the card is charged once.
Toss times out (10 s) or answers 5xx A Payment(failed, failure_code="") is committed. No dunning strike, no email, no status change. Mechanisms 3 + 4. The 02:30 sweep re-queries by order_id: DONE ⇒ repair the row and advance the period; a terminal non-DONE ⇒ stamp the real failure and run the dunning transition then; anything still in flight ⇒ leave it for tomorrow.
A webhook is lost in transit Nothing arrives. An unknown-outcome row sits there. Mechanism 4. Scan (a) never looked at events in the first place.
A webhook is delivered twice Two identical POSTs. Dedup + idempotent repair. dedup_key is unique, so the second insert returns the stored row and the receiver answers duplicate. Even if it processed twice, apply_paid_repair no-ops on an already-paid row.
A webhook arrives from a stranger (no signature to check) An unauthenticated POST with an arbitrary body. Mechanism 5. The body is persisted and then ignored: the repair is gated on a re-query of Toss. A forged payload makes us ask Toss a question, and Toss answers truthfully.
The sweeps run twice on the same day (Celery beat and the worker_ready fan-out at deploy) Two full passes over the due set. The skip rule + the due re-check. Detailed below — this is the open question explainer #16 left for this page.
Two requests for one user race (double-submitted confirm, card change during a sweep) Two transactions want the same subscription. Mechanisms 1 + 2, then the DB. The user-row lock serialises them, the loser re-checks its guards under the lock, and the partial unique constraint is the final backstop.

The sentence to remember, straight out of reconciliation.py's module docstring: money safety is anchored on Payment rows, not events. Events are an optimisation. Rows are the truth.

The error ladder is where the design lives

Four exception classes, each carrying the HTTP status a view should forward:

ExceptionStatusMeansMoney consequence
TossNotConfiguredError503No secret key — dormant. Raised before any network call.Nothing happened.
TossApiError400Toss answered < 500 with a parsed {code, message} — a domain rejection (declined card, bad key).Definitive: no charge. This is what counts as a dunning strike.
TossUnavailableError503Timeout / connection error — or a retryable idempotency code.Unknown. Hand it to reconciliation.
TossBadGatewayError502A 5xx, or a body that would not parse.Unknown. Same lane.

The subtle part is a small frozen set near the top of the file:

# billing/toss.py — RETRYABLE_TOSS_CODES
# Toss idempotency-key codes that mean "the outcome is UNKNOWN, retry to read",
# NOT a domain decline (S7-verified 409/400 taxonomy):
# * IDEMPOTENT_REQUEST_PROCESSING — a duplicate request arrived while the first is
#   still processing (409); the original may still succeed.
# * INVALID_IDEMPOTENCY_KEY — the key was rejected (400, e.g. length/window); the
#   request did not run, but treating it as a counted decline would falsely dun.
RETRYABLE_TOSS_CODES = frozenset(
    {"IDEMPOTENT_REQUEST_PROCESSING", "INVALID_IDEMPOTENCY_KEY"}
)

Both of those arrive as ordinary < 500 responses with a code — exactly the shape that otherwise becomes a TossApiError and a dunning strike. Re-routing them to TossUnavailableError with toss_code=None is what makes the caller write failure_code="", which is what makes reconciliation pick the row up. A single non-empty code here would break that scan and start emailing customers about payments that were merely slow.

Toss calls are never retried in-process, and that is deliberate. Explainer #16 covered core/llm_resilience.py, which retries Gemini calls with exponential backoff and jitter. There is no equivalent here and there should not be. An LLM call is idempotent and cheap to repeat; a charge whose outcome you do not know is a possible double charge. The safe move is to write the unknown marker, return, and let a sweep that can read the outcome decide. Retry-to-read beats retry-to-write whenever money is involved.

2 · Checkout and confirm — billing/services.py

The module docstring is the contract, and it is worth reading before the code:

**Lock-order contract (frozen for S3/S4):** any billing mutation for a user takes
``select_for_update()`` on the ``accounts_user`` row FIRST; BillingKey /
Subscription mutate only under that lock. Toss network calls happen *inside* the
transaction on purpose — the per-user lock only serializes that one user's
concurrent confirms, and ``TOSS_REQUEST_TIMEOUT_SECONDS`` bounds the hold.

``_set_tier`` is the ONLY place ``User.tier`` is written in the billing app. No
JWT token is issued here — the frontend forces a normal refresh after confirm,
and S1's rotation re-stamps the tier claim from the DB.

Two claims in there are worth pausing on, because both look wrong at first glance.

"A network call inside a transaction" is normally an antipattern. Holding a database transaction open across an HTTP round trip means holding locks for as long as a remote server feels like taking. It is defensible here for two specific reasons. The lock is on one user's row, so the only thing that can block is that same user's other billing request — which is exactly what we want blocked. And the hold is bounded: TOSS_REQUEST_TIMEOUT_SECONDS is 10 seconds, so the worst case is a 10-second wait for one person. The alternative — commit an intent, call Toss outside the transaction, commit the result — trades a bounded lock for a distributed-transaction problem, which is a much worse trade.

Nothing re-issues a JWT after the tier flips. A reader coming from explainer #3 already knows why this is safe: the tier claim inside the access token gates nothing server-side. Every enforcement point re-reads the database — accounts/entitlements.py on the backend, and a direct SELECT tier … FROM "backend"."accounts_user" over the read bridge on the agent side. The claim is a display hint for the browser badge, and the frontend's own client library documents it as one. Writing User.tier is therefore sufficient; the refresh is cosmetic timing, not correctness.

start_checkout — three guards and an opaque key

# services.py:283

No charge, no network — it only assembles what the browser SDK needs. The customer_key is a random UUID minted once per user by ensure_billing_profile's get_or_create. Using the user's pk or email instead would be easier and would leak a stable identifier into Toss's systems and into the billing-auth redirect URL; a UUID costs nothing and leaks nothing.

confirm_subscription — twelve numbered steps, one commit

This is the money core. The step comments in the source are numbered 1–12; here is the shape, with the two branches that matter:

assert charge_failure is not None                # 12: committed failure → 400
raise SubscribeChargeFailedError(charge_failure.detail, toss_code=charge_failure.toss_code)

Four things in there repay attention.

  • Step 4 is the replay guard. A double-submitted confirm finds a live subscription and returns it — crucially without re-submitting the authKey, which is single-use.
  • The failure path exits the block normally and raises afterwards. If it raised inside the atomic(), Django would roll back — losing both the failed Payment row and the freshly issued billing key. Leaving the block and raising after the commit keeps the evidence. A decline should be auditable, not erased.
  • Success is one commit. Subscription, Payment and the tier flip land together or not at all. There is no window in which a user is pro without a paid row.
  • _set_tier asserts its own precondition — assert transaction.get_connection().in_atomic_block — so a future caller that forgets the lock fails loudly rather than silently racing.

One honest asymmetry, worth naming. The initial charge's order id is sub-init-{uuid4().hex} — random, not derived. That is fine for the outcome-unknown case (the failed row is committed, carrying the id, so reconciliation can re-query it), but it means the crash-before-commit replay trick from the renewal path does not apply here: if the process dies between Toss's DONE and the commit, nothing is written and the random id is gone with it. The window is narrow — a user-initiated request, with the commit immediately after the call — and it is materially different from the unattended sweep the determinism was designed for. It is still the one gap in the five mechanisms, and this page names it rather than implying it away.

The card-change pair — start_billing_key_checkout / confirm_billing_key_change

Toss has no "update card" call. Changing a card is a second requestBillingAuth → issue round trip that produces a new billing key, and the old one is replaced rather than mutated. That forced a second endpoint pair, because the subscribe pair's guards are exactly backwards for this job: start_checkout refuses when a live subscription exists, and confirm_subscription short-circuits idempotently before the issue call. The money half is the interesting bit, and it is nine lines:

A user whose card was declining has just fixed it; making them wait up to three days for the next retry timer is the churn this path exists to prevent. But the charge does not get its own code path — _retry_outstanding_renewal delegates to renewals.charge_past_due_now, the sweep's own machinery: same renewal_order_id anchor, same existing-Payment skip guard, same bookkeeping, same transitions. There is exactly one charge path in this app, by construction. The outcome is then translated to a three-valued wire field:

# services.py — sweep outcome → the wire's ``retry`` value
_RETRY_OUTCOME_TO_WIRE = {
    "renewed": "paid",
    "past_due": "failed",          # counted decline, still dunning
    "expired": "failed",           # counted decline, final strike
    "outcome_unknown": "failed",   # reconciliation owns the truth
    "skipped_pending": "failed",   # a sweep attempt is already in flight
}

Everything that is not a confirmed payment reports "failed", because from the user's side the amount is still outstanding — and an outcome missing from the map means no charge was attempted at all, so the field is omitted from the response rather than sent as null. An optional field with one honest meaning.

Cancel, reactivate, refund, and a refund request

  • cancel_subscription sets cancel_at_period_end = True. No Toss call, no proration — the sweep already honours the flag. Because it is DB-only it works even while billing is dormant: a user can always exit, even if the keys have been pulled.
  • reactivate_subscription clears the flag and deliberately leaves renewal_attempt_count / next_retry_at alone. Resetting them would let a cancel→reactivate cycle extend past-due grace forever; leaving them means dunning resumes exactly where it paused.
  • refund_payment (operator-only) wraps toss.cancel_payment under the user lock. ALREADY_CANCELED_PAYMENT is treated as confirmed-canceled and proceeds; every other Toss error propagates and rolls back write-free. Then _apply_refund_to_subscription — the exact inverse of the reconciliation repair — expires the subscription and drops the tier to free only when the refunded charge funds the current period (live subscription and no newer paid charge on it). Otherwise the refund is payment-only.
  • file_refund_request (user-facing) files a 환불·청약철회 request and moves no money. It exists because the 전자상거래법 다크패턴 amendment forbids offering 청약철회 only through a channel other than the one used to buy — 해지 and 탈퇴 were already self-serve, 환불 was 고객센터-only. So this is the online filing route, and the actual refund stays the operator's admin action. It takes no user lock at all (no money moves; the only hazard is a double submit, which the partial unique constraint settles at the database) and mails settings.BILLING_REFUND_REQUEST_RECIPIENTS best-effort — an empty list simply means no mail, and a mail failure never fails the filing, because the admin queue is the record.

3 · Renewals and the dunning ladder — billing/renewals.py

The centrepiece. A Celery beat entry (billing.run_renewal_sweep, 02:00 UTC = 11:00 KST) walks every due subscription and drives a state machine whose contract is written out as a table in the module docstring. Four module constants parameterise the whole thing — no Django settings, deliberately:

# billing/renewals.py:97
RENEWAL_MAX_ATTEMPTS = 3
# One offset per resulting count: count→1 waits 1 day, count→2 waits 3 days.
# count→3 (=MAX) expires and never indexes this tuple.
RENEWAL_RETRY_OFFSETS = (timedelta(days=1), timedelta(days=3))
# Retry slack: schedule +1d/+3d *minus* an hour so a retry that lands ~seconds
# after 02:00 is not pushed past the next day's 02:00 sweep.
RETRY_SLACK = timedelta(hours=1)
RECONCILIATION_LOOKBACK_DAYS = 7

The transition table (R1–R9) is the binding contract

def renewal_order_id(subscription: Subscription) -> str:
    period = subscription.current_period_end.astimezone(dt_timezone.utc).strftime("%Y%m%d")
    attempt = subscription.renewal_attempt_count + 1
    return f"sub-{subscription.id}-{period}-a{attempt}"

Three properties, all of them load-bearing:

  • It is a pure function of columns that do not move during an attempt. A crash before commit leaves id, current_period_end and renewal_attempt_count untouched, so tomorrow's sweep recomputes the identical string.
  • It is short — 18 characters against a 64-character column and Toss's 300-character idempotency-key limit.
  • It normalises to UTC before formatting. Note the last measured line: a period ending at 08:00 KST is 23:00 UTC on the previous day and yields 20260930. Unintuitive, but consistent — and consistency is the only property the id needs, since it is never parsed, only recomputed and compared.

The due re-check, measured

The sweep selects candidate ids with a cheap unlocked query, then re-checks each one under the lock — because state can drift between the selection and the lock (a webhook may have repaired the row; a user may have cancelled):

   ACTIVE, period ended yesterday    -> True
   ACTIVE, period ends tomorrow      -> False
   PAST_DUE, retry in 20 h           -> False
   PAST_DUE, retry 1 min ago         -> True
   PAST_DUE, cancel flag set         -> True     ← R2: due in order to EXPIRE, not to charge
   EXPIRED                           -> False

Three rows walked with toy data

R3 — the happy renewal. Subscription 42's period ended at 02:00; no Payment exists for sub-42-20261001-a1; Toss returns a payment object. The success path advances the period from the old end, not from now:

# renewals.py:410 — _apply_renewal_success

sub.current_period_start = old_end
sub.current_period_end = add_months(old_end, 1)

Chaining from the old end is what keeps the anniversary fixed. Advancing from now would let every late sweep slide the billing date forward a few hours a month until a January subscriber is billed in the middle of February. (periods.add_months clamps to the month's last valid day: Jan 31 → Feb 28, or Feb 29 in a leap year, computed with stdlib calendar rather than pulling in python-dateutil, which is only a transitive Celery dependency in the lockfile and must not become a first-class import.)

R4 → R6 → R7 — three declines in a row. Sliced _apply_counted_failure, run three times with a stub subscription and a recorder in place of the mailer:

   strike 1 at 2026-10-01 02:00:03Z -> past_due count=1 status=past_due  next_retry_at=2026-10-02 01:00:03 emails=['past_due'] tier_writes=[]

Exactly two emails across a failing subscription's whole death: one at the first strike (with the retry story) and one at expiry (the downgrade notice). Strike two is silent on purpose — a second "we tried again" mail teaches the customer to ignore the first. Note also tier_writes=[] for the first two strikes: the tier stays pro through the entire grace period, and drops only at expiry, through _expire_subscription.

That expiry write is the app's only one, and rows R1, R2, R7 and the operational unwind all go through it:

# renewals.py:340
def _expire_subscription(locked_user, sub, *, also_save: tuple[str, ...] = ()) -> None:
    """The app's single expiry write: EXPIRED + ``next_retry_at`` NULL + tier→free."""
    sub.status = Subscription.Status.EXPIRED
    sub.next_retry_at = None
    sub.save(update_fields=[*also_save, "status", "next_retry_at", "updated_at"])
    _set_tier(locked_user, str(User.Tier.FREE))

The also_save parameter is a small but instructive piece of craft: row 7 has its own dunning bookkeeping to persist, and passing it through means the whole expiry still commits in one UPDATE — no intermediate state where the status changed but the count did not. And because there is one writer, the model's stated invariant (past_due ⇒ next_retry_at set; active/expired ⇒ NULL) cannot drift.

Why the retry offsets subtract an hour

RETRY_SLACK looks like superstition until you write down the arithmetic. The sweep runs at 02:00 UTC; a charge that fails does so a few seconds later; a naive now + 1 day therefore schedules the retry a few seconds after tomorrow's sweep has already selected its work:

== 4. why RETRY_SLACK subtracts an hour (failure 3 s after the 02:00 sweep starts)
   count=1: raw +1d = 10-02 02:00:03 > that day's sweep 10-02 02:00 -> waits a FULL extra day
            minus RETRY_SLACK = 10-02 01:00:03 <= sweep 10-02 02:00 -> picked up that morning
   count=2: raw +3d = 10-04 02:00:03 > that day's sweep 10-04 02:00 -> waits a FULL extra day
            minus RETRY_SLACK = 10-04 01:00:03 <= sweep 10-04 02:00 -> picked up that morning

Without the hour, a "1-day" retry is a 2-day retry and a "3-day" retry is a 4-day retry — every time, for every customer. The general lesson is worth carrying to any scheduled system: when a timer is consumed by a periodic poller, the timer must land strictly inside the poller's window, not on its boundary.

The skip rule (R9), and the deploy-day double run

Before charging anything, _charge_and_apply asks whether a Payment already exists for the computed order id:

# renewals.py:215 — the first thing the shared charge body does
order_id = renewal_order_id(sub)

existing = Payment.objects.filter(order_id=order_id).first()
if existing is not None:
    age = now - existing.created_at
    if age > timedelta(days=RECONCILIATION_LOOKBACK_DAYS):
        logger.warning("renewal sweep: skipped-pending payment %s is %s old … operator attention", ...)
    return "skipped_pending"

This is the second-layer race guard behind the user lock, and it is what makes an outcome-unknown row (R8) safe: that row was committed, so the next sweep sees it and refuses to charge again, leaving the outcome to reconciliation. The 7-day warning is a nice touch — a pending row older than the reconciliation window means the backstop has itself failed, and only a human can help from there.

The open question from explainer #16, answered. Both billing sweeps are wired twice: as Celery beat entries (02:00 / 02:30 UTC) and in config/celery.py's worker_ready fan-out, which fires on every worker boot. So on a deploy day they run twice — and that is safe, by two independent mechanisms rather than luck. First, _is_due re-checks under the lock: a subscription the first pass renewed now has a current_period_end a month in the future and simply is not due. Second, if the first pass left an unknown outcome instead, the Payment row it committed makes the second pass hit the R9 skip. Neither path can charge twice; the worst case is a second pass that selects nothing and logs a summary of zeroes.

The one seam that expires without charging

expire_subscription_administratively exists for a single operational reason: a billing key issued with sandbox test_ keys cannot be charged with a live_ secret (Toss rejects mixed pairs with INVALID_API_KEY), so a surviving sandbox-era subscription would fail its next renewal, walk the dunning ladder, and email a real person about a payment that never moved money. Its only caller is a management command (manage.py audit_sandbox_pro --unwind --yes). It is deliberately narrow — zero refunds, zero deletes (the audit trail is the point), and silent (no dunning or expiry email, since avoiding exactly those emails is why it exists) — and it reuses _expire_subscription rather than writing its own expiry, so the invariants cannot drift.

4 · Reconciliation — billing/reconciliation.py

02:30 UTC, thirty minutes after the renewal sweep, so an unknown outcome created at 02:00 resolves the same day. Two scans and one shared repair.

The three-way branch is worth reading in full, because "we do not know" is treated as a first-class answer:

TERMINAL_NON_DONE_STATUSES is {ABORTED, CANCELED, EXPIRED, PARTIAL_CANCELED} — an explicit allow-list of "definitely did not settle". Anything unrecognised falls through to "leave it", which is the right default: a status this code has never heard of must not be turned into a customer email.

And when a re-query does resolve an unknown into a real failure, _resolve_as_failure stamps the code and then calls renewals._apply_counted_failure — the same R4/R6/R7 helper. The dunning strike lands a day late, but it lands, with the same emails and the same timers. There is one ladder.

apply_paid_repair — the shared "it settled after all" function

Called from two places (the sweep's row 10 and the webhook's row 14), it opens with an assertion about its own preconditions and an idempotency check:

    "apply_paid_repair must run under the per-user lock"

Then it promotes the row and branches on four situations:

SituationWhat it does
Renewal, subscription still liveAdvance the period from the old end, reset the dunning counter, tier→pro — exactly like a sweep success. Safe because of the skip rule: while this outcome was unknown, no sweep could charge or advance the subscription, so current_period_end is still the period this charge renews.
Renewal, subscription already expiredPromote the Payment and warn — the "paid but expired" corner. A human (or a refund) resolves it.

Notice the shape of the two odd cases. They do not guess, and they do not silently drop money: they make the row truthful, log a warning loudly enough for the operator to find, and stop. Automated repair handles the cases with one obviously-correct answer; everything else escalates.

5 · Webhooks — billing/webhooks.py

Toss's classic webhooks carry no HMAC signature. For most systems that would be alarming; here it is a non-event, and the docstring explains why:

**No webhook secret / signature.** Classic Toss webhooks carry no HMAC signature
(DECOMP finding); Toss's own documented authenticity pattern is exactly this
re-query. Because we trust the payload for *nothing* — dedup keys off content,
and every mutation is gated on an authoritative Toss re-query — a signature would
add no safety.

A signature proves a message came from Toss. A re-query proves the fact came from Toss, which is strictly stronger and also survives a signed-but-stale message. The receiver is therefore unauthenticated, unthrottled, and answers 200 in every path — garbage bodies, duplicates and internal errors included, because Toss retries non-2xx deliveries and the only thing a 5xx would achieve is a retry storm against an endpoint that cannot use the data anyway.

# webhooks.py:43
HANDLED_EVENT_TYPES = frozenset({"PAYMENT_STATUS_CHANGED"})
# Log-only (P71.S11): persisted + marked IGNORED like any unhandled type, but
# worth a log line because we cause them — Toss emits BILLING_DELETED when a
# billing key is deleted, which is exactly what the card-change flow does.
LOGGED_EVENT_TYPES = frozenset({"BILLING_DELETED"})

Deduplication is the other half. compute_dedup_key prefers a Toss-supplied eventId or id; failing that it hashes the canonical tuple (eventType, createdAt, data.paymentKey, data.status); failing even that (a body that did not parse) it hashes the raw bytes. What it never does is key off orderId — with a practical consequence the operations doc records: a test-fire from the Toss dashboard must carry a genuinely unique event, or the receiver correctly reads it back as duplicate.

Processing is then a five-step funnel, and every branch ends in a stamped status:

  1. Not received any more? No-op (not_received) — idempotent by construction.
  2. Unhandled type? ignored.
  3. Re-query Toss by paymentKey, else by orderId. A TossApiError (Toss has no such payment) ends as processed/no_payment; an unreachable Toss leaves the event received on purpose, so scan (b) retries it.
  4. Match the resolved orderId to one of our Payment rows. No match ⇒ ignored.
  5. Repairable only if status == "DONE" and our row is failed with a blank code. Then take the user lock, re-check under it, and call the same apply_paid_repair. Anything else is a no-op success.

The view's last two lines are the final piece of the safety argument:

# views.py — WebhookView.post
try:
    process_webhook_event_task.delay(event.id)
except Exception:  # noqa: BLE001 - enqueue failure: reconciliation scan (b)
    logger.exception("billing webhook: failed to enqueue event %s (left RECEIVED)", event.id)
return Response({"status": "accepted"})

Even the enqueue is allowed to fail. The row stays received, tomorrow's scan (b) re-runs it inline, and Toss still gets its 200. (For how .delay() differs from this codebase's usual transaction.on_commit dispatch, see explainer #16 — here the insert is already committed by the time the view dispatches, because Django runs in autocommit and there is no ATOMIC_REQUESTS.)

7 · The operator surface: django-admin, and nothing else

Explainer #17 walked a real operator console — /api/admin/ routes, an IsOperatorUser permission class, a cached overview snapshot with five summaries. Billing appears in none of it. Measured, not assumed: no module under apps/backend/billing/ imports IsOperatorUser, no /api/admin/ route touches billing, and build_operator_overview_snapshot() has no billing tile.

The operator's actual tools are billing/admin.py (300 lines), and its shape is a decision rather than a gap:

Plan — fully editable

The price-audit surface. Price is data, so the operator can adjust it without a deploy.

Everything else — read-only

Billing keys, subscriptions, payments and webhook events are records, via a ReadOnlyAdminMixin. The raw billing_key string appears only in a detail view's readonly_fields, never in a list column.

RefundRequest — the work queue

The one narrow exception: its status is editable, because that model is the operator's queue. The content stays read-only.

The refund action — superusers only

PaymentAdmin.refund_selected_payment is gated by has_refund_permission → request.user.is_superuser. It refuses unless exactly one row is selected, stamps a fixed reason ("고객 요청에 따른 운영자 환불" — no free text at the admin), and reports through message_user, never a 500.

Why this is a reasonable shape for a one-plan product: every operator action here is rare (adjust a price, work a refund queue, execute a refund) and every one of them is dangerous in a way the console's routine tiles are not. Django admin gives per-model permissions, an audit log and a superuser gate for free — building the same thing as REST routes plus console UI would be more code and more attack surface for a workflow measured in a handful of actions a month.

What it costs is also real, and worth stating: there is no billing tile on the operator overview (so nobody notices a spike in declines without going looking), the sweeps' outcomes live in worker logs rather than a dashboard, and the two loud logger.warning corners from apply_paid_repair — "paid but expired" and "orphan paid initial" — surface only to whoever reads logs. For a product with one plan and few subscribers that is a fair trade; it is the kind of trade worth revisiting the day the numbers change.

Quiz

Five questions. Click an option for immediate feedback.

1. A renewal sweep charges subscription 42, Toss returns DONE, and the worker is killed before the transaction commits. Tomorrow's sweep runs. What stops the customer being charged twice?

The rollback is the point: because nothing moved, the order id sub-42-{period_end}-a{count+1} recomputes to the same string, and Toss deduplicates on it. A successful billing charge sends no webhook at all, and a crashed worker's locks are released by the database.

2. The Toss webhook receiver is unauthenticated, verifies no signature, and answers 200 to literally any body. Why is that not a security hole here?

A forged payload can at most make the server ask Toss a question. Toss answers truthfully, and a payment that is not DONE repairs nothing. (The dedup key deliberately ignores orderId, and the repair runs under the ordinary per-user lock.)

3. A renewal charge raises TossUnavailableError. What is written, and who resolves it?

The empty failure_code is the "outcome unknown" marker and the only thing reconciliation's scan (a) keys on. A real code would mean a definitive decline — and would silently exempt the row from ever being re-checked.

4. RENEWAL_RETRY_OFFSETS is (1 day, 3 days) but the code schedules now + offset - RETRY_SLACK, an hour earlier. Why?

It is boundary arithmetic: a timer consumed by a periodic poller must land strictly inside the poller's window. Without the slack, every advertised retry interval silently gains a full day.

5. A user's card declined once, so their subscription is past_due. They try to subscribe again from the pricing page. What happens, and why is it built that way?

Allowing a re-subscribe would create a second live subscription and a second monthly charge for one person. The constraint ~Q(status="expired") makes that impossible at the database, and P71's card-change pair is the designed way out of past_due.
