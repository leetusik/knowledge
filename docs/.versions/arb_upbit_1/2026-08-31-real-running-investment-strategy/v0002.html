Background — the event, and the inequality that shaped everything

None of this is a view on XRP. The whole system exists to harvest one temporary promotion, and it would be pointless without it.

Upbit's 메이커 플러스+ event (notice 1793218405) runs 2026-08-29 00:00 → 2026-09-11 23:59 KST across the BTC and USDT markets. For orders placed through the API it pays:

ItemValueWhat it means here
Maker fee (API)0.00 %A resting order that gets filled costs nothing.
Taker fee, event markets0.05 %Crossing the spread costs 5 bps.
Maker reward+0.04 %Of filled notional, paid in the quote currency (USDT here).
Withholding22 %Of the whole gross reward, once gross passes ₩50,000. A cliff, not a rate → net reward 0.0312 % (3.12 bps) above it.
KRW-market taker0.05 %The KRW market is not in the event.

That last row is the load-bearing one. The obvious design for an event like this is quote both sides of a thin event pair and hedge each fill on the deep KRW market. Price it out on the real book:

hedge one fill on KRW-XRP:
    taker fee        5.00 bps
    half spread      2.81 bps      (measured, research/data/quality_*.csv)
    slippage         1.00 bps
                   ─────────
                     8.81 bps   per hedged fill

what the fill earns:  3.12 bps   (0.04% reward, after the 22% cliff)

The hedge-cost fact. A KRW taker hedge costs 2.8× what the reward pays. Any design that hedges per fill pays away its entire reward and more, on every single trade. So profit has to come from maker-to-maker round trips inside the USDT market — buy passively low, sell passively high, and never cross the spread. Hedging is reserved for a band edge or a kill-switch unwind. The live configuration has band_mode: "soft" and hedge_cost_krw = 0: it has never hedged.

Two more constraints fall out of the same notice and never move:

  • Never self-match or wash. It forfeits the whole reward. Hence: one order per side, and a bot that structurally cannot cross itself.
  • Eligibility can be revoked. A 거래유의 (investment-warning) flag removes a pair from the event — BTC-SNX was flagged mid-research. So the bot re-checks orders/chance hourly and hard-stops if the maker fee is no longer 0 or the market goes non-active.

Capital is ₩2,562,811 — the KRW that was already on the account, not a funding decision. At that size the projected gross reward stays under the ₩50,000 cliff, so in practice the 22 % has not applied; the code still models the cliff correctly because a good run could cross it.

Intuition — fair price, the δ stand-off, and the band that is secretly the size knob

1. Where "fair" comes from when the book you quote is thin

USDT-XRP is the only event pair with a spread worth capturing — but it is thin, and its own mid is noisy. The KRW books behind it are enormous. So fair value is imported:

f = last(KRW-XRP) / last(KRW-USDT) — never candles, always trade ticks or book mids. But f is systematically off: the event pair's own mid ran at +3.18 bps against it on average. That level is real and persistent, so an EWMA with a 1,800 s half-life tracks own_mid/f − 1 and corrects it away: f' = f·(1+b). What is left is ≈4.15 bps of σ — irreducible noise.

Why the half-life is 1,800 s and not 300 s. The 300 s point scored 55 % higher core alpha in the sweep (₩407,537 vs ₩263,046) and was disqualified anyway: its inv_share was 1.50, meaning the "profit" was larger than the strategy's own output and therefore really inventory drift. A fast EWMA chases the market's own mid, which turns anchored quoting into directional trading wearing a costume.

2. Why δ = 14 bps and not 6

δ is how far outside f' the quotes sit. Set it too tight and every fill is a pick-off: you are trading against the anchor's own noise, not against real flow.

anchor residual σ            4.15 bps
δ = 14 bps                =  3.4 σ
one tick (0.001 USDT)     =  7.23 bps   →  δ ≈ 1.9 ticks
market spread             = 14.5 bps    →  we quote about the width of the book

The sweep found core alpha monotone increasing in δ over 6 / 8 / 10 / 14 bps (₩209,763 → ₩263,046), and 14 was the grid edge. That monotonicity is the reason δ was left as a runtime lever rather than frozen: if competition tightens the book, a lower δ still works (₩209,763 at δ = 6, because fill pace rises to 25.7/day) — you just move along the curve.

3. The post-fill band — the one non-obvious mechanism in the whole system

Inventory is tracked as q = XRP holdings valued in KRW ÷ capital. You want q ≈ target. Two separate mechanisms push it there, and the second one has a consequence nobody predicted.

(a) The lean. Both quotes shift by k·(q − target) with k = 2δ. Long → both prices drop → your ask is more attractive and your bid less. This is textbook inventory skew.

(b) The post-fill band. Before an order is placed at all, the engine asks: if this order filled completely, would inventory still be inside the band? If not, that side is simply not quoted.

dev       = q − target
size_frac = order notional / capital

bid allowed   iff   dev + size_frac ≤ band
ask allowed   iff   size_frac − dev ≤ band

The consequence: band is the order-size ceiling. Put dev = 0 into either line and you get size_frac ≤ band. An order larger than the band is structurally unplaceable — not rejected by the exchange, never constructed. This is why raising size_pct alone did nothing for months: the P1 grid held band = 0.10 and swept size up to 30 %, so every row above 10 was a dead run producing zeros. Full size was unlocked by raising the band with the size, which is exactly what P4's 912-run band-matched sweep was for.

And a second consequence: both sides are quotable simultaneously only while |dev| ≤ band − size_frac. That is a two-sided zone of width 2(band − size_frac). At the live set (band 0.25, size 0.20) the zone is 0.10 wide: q ∈ [0.20, 0.30]. Outside it, one side goes quiet and waits.

Read this before you "fix" a skewed inventory at 3 a.m. A one-sided book is the design working, not a fault. The impaired side is degraded, not dead — it keeps quoting at reduced size. P6 priced an automatic rebalance: one taker leg ≈ 8.79 bps ≈ ₩819, and since the layout holds no KRW it is structurally two legs ≈ ₩1,600, against ≈ ₩71.5 of core alpha per forgone fill. 48 backtested rule variants: 0 of 48 beat their own baseline on core alpha. The bot does the cheaper thing — it waits.

4. Why the ranking key is core_alpha and not PnL

A control that just held the capital split without quoting at all returned +1.31 % / 14 d, and the USDT-SOL split +4.49 %. Rank on net PnL and pure directional drift wins the tournament. So (ADR D2):

core_alpha = trading_pnl + reward_net − hedge_cost        # marks on held inventory EXCLUDED
inv_share  = |inventory marks| / max(|core_alpha|, 1)     # disqualify above 0.5

inv_share is the honesty check. It is what killed the 300 s basis half-life, the wider bands, and both two-leg combos (inv_share 1.00–1.18 — the entire "alpha" was drift). It is also why P6's rebalance rule looked like a 138.9 % improvement on net PnL and was still rejected: that gain was inventory_pnl, i.e. one lucky XRP move over one 36 h window.

Code — how it was established, and how it runs

Part 1 — how the numbers were established

Upbit publishes no historical order book. That single gap dictates the whole pipeline: you cannot backtest passive fills against data you can buy, so you have to record it yourself first, and recorder uptime literally is the calibration budget (~0.92 GB/day).

The scan, and why there is exactly one candidate

arb.scan walked all 561 BTC/USDT event pairs. Almost all of them have no spread to capture:

Eight other candidate designs were built and dropped — touch quoting on USDT-BTC and USDT-ETH (their apparent 13.6 bps/fill edge is an artefact of marking a fill against fair on a 0.81 bps book), a BTC-USDT mirror, stablecoin pairs, tier-B alts, two combos. Every rejection is in research/recommendation.md §3 with the number that killed it.

The three things the backtest cannot tell you

This matters more than any single result, because it is the reason the staging ladder exists:

  1. The full-fill rule is optimistic. A print trading through our resting price fills the whole order. The median USDT-XRP print is ₩50k against a ₩1M order, so modelled notional per fill is roughly 2× real.
  2. Implied market share is implausible. Model turnover was 8.8 % of the pair's entire daily traded value.
  3. Adverse selection is not modelled beyond a small pick-off term. The 5 bps haircut in the base case is an assumption.

So the honest claim was never "this earns 2.38 %". It was: the edge per fill is measured (16.7 bps against fair on a 14.5 bps book); the fill pace and the adverse selection are not, and only a live paper run can measure them. Which it did: 19.2 h of paper coverage, 4.99 fills/day, adverse selection −14.3 bps at 30 s — negative, i.e. in our favour.

Part 2 — how it runs

Three long-running host processes, plus one opt-in fourth. None supervises another; the recorder and the bot each hold their own sockets and their own slice of Upbit's rate limits.

The quote path, verified against a real log line

src/arb/live/quote.py is deliberately pure — no I/O, no clock, no asyncio — and is a line-by-line transcription of arb.backtest.engine. Where the two could disagree, the backtest wins. Here is the arithmetic, and then the same arithmetic checked against the log:

# src/arb/live/quote.py — desired_quotes(), the strategy step
q    = inventory_q(hold_xrp, px_krw_xrp, p.capital_krw)   # XRP in KRW / capital
dev  = q - p.target
lean = p.k_frac * dev                                     # k = 2δ
bid, ask = _clip_post_only(f * (1.0 - p.delta - lean),
                           f * (1.0 + p.delta - lean),
                           s.best_bid, s.best_ask, p.tick_f)
10:43:44 KST  f'=1.346596  q=0.3410  resting=[ask@1.349]

dev  = 0.3407 − 0.25          = 0.0907
lean = 2 × 0.0014 × 0.0907    = 0.000254        (2.54 bps, both quotes shift down)
ask  = 1.346596 × (1 + 0.0014 − 0.000254)
     = 1.3481392  →  ceil to the 0.001 tick  →  1.349     ✓ matches the log
bid  = 1.346596 × (1 − 0.0014 − 0.000254) = 1.344369 → floor → 1.344
       but  dev + size_frac = 0.0907 + 0.20 = 0.29 > band 0.25  →  refused, reason "band"

Three live-only refinements sit on top of that shared arithmetic, and each has a reason worth remembering:

  • Exact decimals, not binary floats. Prices are quantized with Decimal; volume is floored onto the exchange's 8-dp lattice, so the balance cap can never be exceeded by rounding.
  • Post-only clipping. bid ≤ best_ask − 1 tick, ask ≥ best_bid + 1 tick, so a quote can never be born a taker.
  • BAND_TOL = 1e-3 — the one place the live path deliberately diverges from the backtest. The band values an order at price × KRW-USDT while q is marked at KRW-XRP, so a holdings-capped order lands 2–9 × 10⁻⁵ of capital outside the band and, with target == band, could never be placed at all. That knife edge left the live run bid-only until P4.F1. engine._band_ok keeps no tolerance, because the backtest must stay bit-reproducible.

Risk: three outcomes, and the exit-code contract

src/arb/live/risk.py returns exactly one of three things, and the loop does precisely what it says:

OutcomeMeaningTriggers
quoteeverything inside its limit—
pauserecoverable: cancel all, keep the process alive, resume after 2 s of everything being freshanchor or own-book staleness, a public socket down, (live) the private socket down
stopterminal: cancel all, confirm flat, exit 2daily core loss > ₩100,000 in a KST day · HTTP 418 · eligibility check fails · a second unexplained resting order · (live only) trailing-24 h adverse selection > 10 bps on ≥ 10 fills

Note what is absent: there is no inventory rule in risk.py. The post-fill band already blocks the growing side by construction, so this module only reports an XRP share above target + band as a warning. A limit enforced structurally does not need a second, weaker enforcement watching it.

The exit code is the whole contract between the bot and everything above it:

CodeMeaningSupervisor
0clean stop — duration elapsed, SIGTERM/SIGINTstops too
1crash (and any other unexpected code)restarts after 10 s, ≤ 5 per rolling hour
2risk stop — flat, deliberatenever restarts
3refused — another bot owns data/live/bot.pidnever restarts

The order path: four rules a full-size two-sided run needs

These four defects existed only on real, two-sided, full-size wiring — none of them can appear in paper mode, which is precisely why paper mode is not sufficient evidence:

  1. The cancel→place interlock. An ack is not confirmation: Upbit releases the locked currency ≈500 ms after acknowledging a cancel. So _do_place waits in _await_side_clear until the outstanding cancel is confirmed gone, capped at 2 s, after which it proceeds with a cancel_unconfirmed warning. Without it, a re-quote raced its own cancel and got a 400 insufficient_funds.
  2. The reject memo arms only on a post-only refusal. It used to arm on a funding 400, which suppressed a whole side for 72 s.
  3. Fills book newly executed volume only, keyed on (order, cumulative executed_volume) — a repeated message used to double-count.
  4. A still-resting order is never marked cancelled, which would free a side that really has an order on it.

The ledger, and why exactly one process writes it

data/live/ledger.sqlite (WAL, schema v1, 8 tables) is the only record of what the bot did, and it cannot be regenerated. Every reader — the report, the dashboard, the manager, the staging gate — opens it readonly=True. That is not politeness; SQLite's WAL mode allows many readers but exactly one writer, and the wal-index lives in shared memory, so all processes must be on the same host. P7 learned the second half of that sentence the hard way: over a Docker bind mount host and container do not share POSIX locks, so a host read-write open believes it is alone, checkpoints, and unlinks the container writer's WAL.

The P8 manager — where an LLM does and does not earn its place

arb.manager is an opt-in 5-minute loop: gather (read-only) → decide (pure) → act + audit. It exists to buy exactly two things: uptime, and applying one dated decision on time.

# src/arb/manager/core.py — decide(), in order
1. bot pid alive but heartbeat > 120 s   → KILL (SIGTERM, SIGKILL after 30 s)
2. bot down, supervisor up               → OK   (its own restart loop owns this)
3. both down — what killed it decides:
     exit 3                              → LEAVE_DOWN            (never acted on)
     exit 2, risk ≠ http_418             → LEAVE_DOWN_ALERT      (operator decision)
     exit 2, http_418, cooled 900 s      → ask triage
     supervisor budget exhausted         → ask triage
     ≥ 3 consecutive exit 1              → ask triage
     exit 0 within 900 s of our own kill → RESTART (manager-induced)
     exit 0 otherwise                    → LEAVE_DOWN  (never undo a deliberate stop)
     anything else                       → RESTART

The LLM (Gemini) is consulted for one question, the only input in the whole system that is text rather than a number: this bot is down and the log is ambiguous — restart it, or leave it down? It answers one of three words — restart / leave_down / leave_down_alert — and that answer is a vote that the deterministic caps then check. Every failure mode (missing key, timeout, HTTP error, unparsable body, an action outside the closed set) returns leave_down: leaving the bot down is always the safe answer, so the fallback is the design. Log lines matching key|jwt|bearer|authorization|secret|token|password are scrubbed before the prompt is built.

What the manager cannot do — as a property, not a policy. The only commands the package can construct are bin/bot.sh start, bin/bot.sh stop and the report. Not the kill switch, not convert --unwind, not convert --execute, no change to capital / band / size / target, no ledger write. Hard caps: 3 lifecycle actions per KST day, applied after any LLM vote.

The narrowness is the point. δ adaptation was already a dated deterministic rule; rebalancing was already measured as unprofitable. Handing either to a model would have added variance, not return. The return path is uptime plus one decision on time — not cleverness.

The one decision still ahead: ADR D18

δ = 14 bps was confirmed against a book that trades 1 tick wide (≈7 bps) much of the day — 57.2 % of heartbeats on the last full report, 66–70 % on earlier windows. If we are standing off 14 bps from a 7 bps book, we may simply be too far out to get filled. So the question was given a date and a rule rather than an opinion:

# src/arb/manager/core.py
D18_DUE          = 2026-09-01 22:00 KST     # 48 h on the full stage
D18_FILLS_PER_DAY = 4.0
D18_DELTA_BPS     = 11.0

if fills_per_day < 4:  restart with  bin/bot.sh start --stage full --delta-bps 11

research/live/xrp_anchor.json is not edited — the file keeps recording the confirmed set, and the change is expressed as a runtime override. δ is read at start only, so the checkpoint costs exactly one restart of a live run. If the manager is up when the clock strikes, it applies this itself and writes data/live/manager/delta.json; if it is down, the checkpoint falls to you by hand.

The daily loop

make stack-status                                  # recorder, bot, dashboard, manager — always exit 0
uv run python -m arb.live.report --stage full      # → data/live/reports/  (writes a gate event)
open http://127.0.0.1:8777/                        # also http://100.77.164.42:8777/ on the tailnet
df -h .                                            # ~0.92 GB/day of raw recording

Four rules that only bite once real orders rest.

  • Never make stack-down during the event — it stops the bot and ends the run. make dash-stop for the page alone; make -n stack-down to see what the real one would do.
  • Never edit bin/bot.sh while it runs. Bash reads a script by byte offset, so rewriting it in place makes the running loop resume at a stale offset and die with a syntax error on a line that does not exist. Observed on 2026-08-30.
  • preflight cannot pass while the bot quotes — its no resting orders check FAILs by design. Run it after bin/bot.sh kill.
  • The Mac must stay awake with the lid open. caffeinate -i (which the supervisor wraps itself in) holds off idle sleep only; closing the lid still sleeps the machine and abandons real resting orders.

Best practices & next steps

Where this aligns with prevailing practice

Quoting around a reference price, skewed by inventory, is the standard design. The Avellaneda–Stoikov framework — the reference point for essentially every open-source market maker — starts from the same insight: quote around a reservation price, the mid shifted against your current inventory, so that "at q > 0, the trader is long, lowering the reservation price to favor sell orders; at q < 0, the trader is short, raising the reservation price to favor buy orders" (Hummingbot's guide to the Avellaneda & Stoikov strategy — hummingbot.org). Our lean = k·(q − target) with k = 2δ is exactly that shift, and our target is their inventory-target parameter. The difference is that theirs is derived from a risk-aversion coefficient γ and a volatility estimate, while ours was swept: k = 2δ is a grid result, not a closed-form.

One writer per SQLite database is the documented rule, not a stylistic choice. SQLite's own WAL documentation states plainly that "since there is only one WAL file, there can only be one writer at a time", and that "all processes using a database must be on the same host computer; WAL does not work over a network filesystem… because WAL requires all processes to share a small amount of memory" (Write-Ahead Logging — sqlite.org). ADR D11's single-writer ledger is the correct reading of that; P7's discovery that a Docker bind mount breaks lock sharing between host and container is the same constraint arriving through a door nobody was watching.

Restart-on-failure with a retry budget is the conventional supervision shape. Docker's own restart policies offer on-failure, which restarts "if it exits due to an error, which manifests as a non-zero exit code", with a :max-retries limit (Start containers automatically — docs.docker.com). bin/bot.sh __supervise implements that shape by hand — restart on exit 1, at most 5 per rolling hour — which is also why the compose file deliberately sets restart: "no": two supervisors with different opinions about a risk stop is worse than one.

Where this deliberately diverges — and why that is defensible

  • No per-fill hedge, and no automatic rebalance at all. Every conventional market maker hedges or rebalances inventory. Here both are refused on measured cost: the hedge is 2.8× the reward, and 48 backtested rebalance variants produced 0 wins on core alpha. The divergence is legitimate because the venue's fee asymmetry — the reward market and the deep hedging market are different markets with different fee schedules — is unusual and temporary.
  • Restart-on-failure, but explicitly not on a risk stop. Docker's on-failure would happily restart a bot that stopped because it lost ₩100,000 today. The exit-code contract splits "crashed" (exit 1, restartable) from "decided to stop" (exit 2, never), and that distinction does not exist in generic supervisors. It has to be encoded in the application.
  • An LLM with one question and three legal answers. The prevailing direction of travel is toward broader agent autonomy. This system went the other way on purpose: everything that could be a dated rule or a measured number was made one, and the model was left with the single judgment that is genuinely textual. That is a smaller LLM footprint than the field default, and the justification is written down as ADR D21 rather than assumed.

Next steps

  1. Take the D18 checkpoint deliberately, and record which surface you read. It is due ≈2026-09-01 22:00 KST. The window is the full-stage latest session — the same one the report, the gate and the dashboard share. Do not compute a second window; a second window is a second answer.
  2. Close the alerting gap. If the manager leaves the bot down, the only surfaces that say so are an audit line, make stack-status, and a log file. The dashboard says nothing about the manager at all. A phone-visible alert is the highest-value small addition left, and it is cheap: the audit trail is already one JSON line per tick.
  3. Decide the manager's post-event status explicitly. The standing authorisation for an agent to restart the bot was written for an unattended run. When you are back at the keyboard, either renew it in writing or stop the manager — do not let it lapse into ambiguity.
  4. Run the cutover after 2026-09-11, not before. RUNBOOK.md §9 is written and rehearsed in parts. Uptime is itself return, so mid-event it is pure downside; afterwards it is free.

Quiz

Five questions. Click an answer to see whether it is right and why.

1. The live set has band = 0.25 and size_pct = 20. You want bigger orders, so you raise size_pct to 30 and leave everything else alone. What happens?

The post-fill band requires size_frac ≤ band even at dev = 0. At size_frac = 0.30 > band = 0.25 no order passes the test, so none is ever constructed — the exchange never sees anything to reject. This is exactly the trap that made every row above size_pct = 10 in the P1 grid a dead run.

2. A backtested variant shows net PnL up 138.9 % against its baseline, with the entire gain in inventory_pnl. Why was it rejected anyway?

ADR D2. inventory_pnl is the mark on held inventory, i.e. what the price did while you were holding — luck, not strategy output. Rank on net PnL and pure drift tops the table. core_alpha = trading_pnl + reward_net − hedge_cost excludes marks by construction, and inv_share > 0.5 disqualifies a candidate whose "alpha" is really drift. On core alpha, 0 of 48 rebalance points beat baseline.

3. The manager may restart a bot that stopped with exit 2 for http_418, after a 900 s cool-down and if triage agrees. It may never restart one that stopped with exit 2 for the daily loss stop. What is the principle?

The split is between conditions that heal and conditions that mean something. Waiting out a 418 changes the world; restarting after a loss stop changes nothing except that you are losing money again. Everything unrecognised is treated as terminal too — the default answer is always "leave it down".

4. Why is the ADR D18 δ change applied as --delta-bps 11 at a restart, instead of editing research/live/xrp_anchor.json or hot-reloading it?

Two reasons stacked. First, provenance: xrp_anchor.json is the set the operator confirmed on 2026-08-30, and an experimental δ has no business overwriting that record. Second, mechanism: nothing in the bot hot-reloads — parameters are read once at start — so a restart is the only way to change one, and the supervisor makes a restart cheap. The checkpoint therefore costs exactly one restart.

5. The fair price is last(KRW-XRP) / last(KRW-USDT) from trade ticks or book mids — never candles. And the basis EWMA half-life is 1,800 s even though 300 s scored 55 % higher. What do those two choices have in common?

A candle is an aggregate that lags and smooths; a 300 s EWMA chases the thin pair's own mid closely enough that f' stops being an independent reference. In both cases you lose the thing the anchor was for — an opinion about fair value that the thin book did not supply. The 300 s point's inv_share of 1.50 is that failure showing up in the metrics: more "profit" than strategy output, i.e. drift.
