1. Background

1.1 The system in one picture

If you already know how a report becomes a card on the research page, skip to 1.2.

The IB research page follows what investment banks and asset managers publish. In this codebase each of them is a house: HSBC US, Merrill, Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, Jefferies, Vanguard and a few more. Houses publish reports: articles, PDFs and podcast pages. A chain of programs turns those reports into the page. Each step below shows its real numbers for the week of 10/5.

collectfinds report links and dates on each house's public pages and registers them10/5 week: 8 reports, 6 houses
→
summarizecaptures each report and writes a Korean summarya report reaches the lists only after this step
→
extract signalsan LLM reads each report and records the house's views, with evidence checked against the textone model call per report
→

→
aggregate weeklycounts the votes and judges rising, cooling and conflictsno model call; six tables
→
API → pagethe backend serves the tables; the frontend draws 이번 주 시그널production on 10/5: 1 / 0 / 1

The first five steps live in the pipeline (ib-research-report-pipeline/ in the glide-ilab repo). The backend (dashboard-backend, a Django app) reads the pipeline's tables and serves the research API. The frontend (withdrawal-planner-fe, a Next.js app) draws the screens under /dev/global-ib-research. One fact shaped how this phase handled data: the backend's dev and production servers share one database. A data change is live for both the moment it is written; only code changes wait for a deploy.

Four words used everywhere below

  • Signal: one house's view on one theme over one horizon (short, mid or long), with a stance (bull, bear or neutral). Each signal carries a short evidence quote that a program checks against the report.
  • Theme: a named topic in the theme dictionary, such as 기업 실적·이익 (corporate earnings). Each theme belongs to a group, such as 섹터.
  • Vote: a house counts at most once a week for each theme and horizon, however many reports it publishes.
  • Week: Monday to Sunday, Korean time (KST). "The 10/5 week" runs from Monday 10/5 to Sunday 10/11.

1.2 The theme dictionary, and half the signals in limbo

The extractor does not invent the page's vocabulary. It maps each signal to a theme in a dictionary table, research_themes. Every theme there is in one of four states, and the state decides where its signals can appear:

activeReviewed, with a Korean name and a group. Counted by the aggregation and shown on the page.
candidateCreated automatically when a signal matched no active theme. Not reviewed: the name is the model's own, and several candidates often mean the same thing.
mergedPoints at another theme through merged_into. Its signals count under that theme.
retiredHidden everywhere. Its signals never show.

The signal backfill loaded into production on 10/15 (1,457 signals from 6 runs) brought 422 candidate themes with it. Some signals are eligible to count: they come from registered houses, were accepted, and were extracted with enough confidence. Of those, 511 sat on active themes and 514 on candidates. The aggregation writes rows only for active themes, so half of the usable signals never reached 이번 주 시그널.

The opposite problem showed on the screens. The backend hid only retired themes, so candidate labels did appear: as chips on the report list, as cards on report pages, and on theme pages whose trend lines were all zeros (no aggregate rows exist for candidates). The review that approved the backend's first research pull request asked for candidates to be hidden before the production release.

1.3 What 이번 주 시그널 is for

The section has three lists:

  • 떠오르는 테마 (rising): up to five cards;
  • 식는 테마 (cooling): up to five;
  • 갈리는 테마 (conflicts): up to three.

The signed design shows them full, 5/5/3. On 10/15, production showed the 10/5 week as 1/0/1: one rising theme (기업 실적·이익), no cooling theme, and one conflict (인플레이션 재가속, short horizon).

Before changing anything, the phase's first research step pinned down what the section is for, from the roadmap and the signed design. Its answer: it says what changed this week. That means themes turning up, themes turning down, and themes that split the houses, each judged as of the latest week. Five constraints follow from that purpose, and everything after was judged against them:

  1. No status that this week's run contradicts: nothing stale passed off as news.
  2. No cooling caused by a house falling silent: a theme cools only when houses that are still publishing talk about it less.
  3. Conflicts use the 8-week window. That window is a roadmap rule, so changing it is an explicit ask.
  4. 신규 (new) only for themes that really emerged.
  5. Thin weeks stay honest: show what exists, and don't pad it.

You added the phase's own version of this rule at its start: "not only fill the 이번주 시그널, but make it reasonable, and not cross purpose of it." Filling the counts was never enough.

2. Intuition

2.1 One house, one vote

Everything on the page starts from one counting rule. A house gets at most one vote a week for each theme and horizon. If House X publishes three reports in a week that all say something long-term about Theme A, that is still one vote. This keeps a prolific house from drowning out the rest. Three tests are then run on the votes.

Rising needs two things over the last four weeks:

  • the votes trend upward: the least-squares slope is positive. For four weeks it is (−3·y₀ − y₁ + y₂ + 3·y₃) / 10;
  • at least two houses voted in those four weeks.

Take 기업 실적·이익 in the 10/5 week. Its votes ran 1, 2, 1, 3 over 9/14 to 10/5, from four houses. The slope is (−3 − 2 + 1 + 9) / 10 = +0.5, so it rises.

Cooling compares the last 4 weeks with the last 12. A theme cools when two things hold:

  • its 12-week average is at least a floor: 1.0 vote a week today, and 0.5 in the proposal;
  • its 4-week average is at most half its 12-week average.

The floor exists so that a theme that was barely discussed cannot "cool". It also explains why production almost never showed cooling. A floor of 1.0 means at least 12 votes in 12 weeks, and few themes get that many. Here is a toy theme to make that concrete:

A conflict looks at the last 8 weeks, one theme and one horizon at a time:

  • each house takes its most frequent stance in those weeks. Neutral and a tie take no side;
  • a conflict needs at least one bull house, at least one bear house and at least two reports in total.

A short-term bull against a long-term bear is not a conflict, because the two houses are talking about different horizons.

The windows (4, 12 and 8 weeks) and the thresholds come from R1, the aggregation rules 대표 approved. The code labels each value either 규칙 (a rule fixed by a source such as the roadmap) or 출발값 (a starting value chosen for the stage). Either way, a change is an ask, not a quiet edit. That is why the cooling floor appears in the proposal as a question.

2.2 Three leaks and one thing that was not a leak

Why did the 10/5 week show only one rising theme? Following a week's reports from publication to the page, the phase found three places where real material leaked away.

  1. The dictionary leak. Half the usable signals sat on candidates (1.2). The aggregation ignores candidates, so those votes never counted.
  2. The floor leak. At 1.0 vote a week, cooling almost never fires. Over 26 weeks production showed cooling twice (26-week totals: 107 rising, 2 cooling, 114 conflicts).
  3. The collection leak. The registered houses published about 17 in-scope reports in the 10/5 week; we collected 8. Where the rest went:
    • HSBC US, the biggest single gap, looked silent but wasn't. Its October monthly went up on 10/9, and the 10/11 run found nothing on the listing. A special report from 9/17 also went uncollected (2.6 explains both).
    • Merrill's 10/11 crawl failed outright on timeouts, missing its 10/5 weekly market outlook (CMO).
    • Morgan Stanley's Thoughts on the Market (about five a week, with full transcripts) is excluded by design.
    • Goldman Sachs: a topic allowlist drops its Markets articles.

Not a leak: extraction. In the last four weeks, 17 reports produced no signals:

  • 11 had nothing to extract;
  • 2 were missed, both Merrill CMOs whose extraction input held only the web teaser, not the linked PDF;
  • 3 were borderline;
  • 1 was a failure.

No prompt or threshold change would raise the yield truthfully. The 10/5 week is thin because few reports came in, not because the model read them badly.

2.3 Filling honestly: what was tried and turned down

The easy way to fill a page is to loosen the rules until it is full. The phase judged each variant by the lists it would show, not by its counts, and asked of every item: is this really this week's news? Here is what passed, with the figures from the local copies:

And here is what was turned down, each for a reason you can check against the five constraints in 1.3:

  • A four-week view (each theme's latest status within four weeks). It would show 15 rising items. But by 10/5, 13 of them had already been re-judged stable with falling votes, such as AI 자본지출 at 3·2·1·0. That is stale news shown as this week's.
  • A 12-week conflict window. It shows pairs 7 to 11 weeks old and pushes this week's real pair down the list. The 8-week window is also a roadmap rule.
  • A looser cooling ratio (two-thirds instead of half). Its only addition at 10/5, 미국증시 전망 at −40 %, is noise.
  • A lower floor (0.34 or 0.25). These give 75 or 183 coolings over 26 weeks, mostly chance (see the callout).
  • Rising with a single house. It shows 14 rising themes in one week: one house's report would be enough to make a "trend".

Why a low floor fills the page with noise. Take a theme that got 3 votes in 8 weeks, about 0.375 a week. If nothing changed at all, the chance it gets 0 votes in the next four weeks is about e−1.5 ≈ 0.22. About 49 themes sit at that level, so roughly 10 of them would "cool" in any given week by luck alone. The proposed floor of 0.5 sits near the edge: 6 votes in 8 weeks followed by 4 silent weeks happens by chance only about 5 % of the time (e−3).

2.4 The dictionary supplement

The biggest honest gain was to review the candidates and fold them into the dictionary. A gap list from 10/15 proposed new themes and aliases for the most-used candidates. Before writing the migration, the phase double-checked it item by item, with one strict test. An item fails if either:

  • its stance would flip on the target, or
  • its subject differs from the target's: a cause, an effect, a neighbouring market, or a relative regional call.

A failed item stays a candidate. Nothing was rerouted to some other theme, and nothing was invented.

What a sign flip looks like (a toy example). Say a candidate is "Treasury yields rise", and a house is bull on it: it expects yields to go up. Merge that candidate into a theme called 국채금리 하락 ("Treasury yields fall"), and the same vote now reads as bullish on falling yields. The house's view is reversed. Checks like this are why some aliases stayed out.

Real examples of items that stayed candidates:

  • Venezuelan oil production was not merged into the oil outlook;
  • a Bank of Japan hold was not merged into Japan's policy normalization;
  • AI ecosystem valuations were not merged into the AI bubble;
  • two whole proposed themes, AI 고용 영향 and 관세·통상정책, stayed candidates, because their members used opposite stance conventions.

The result, as migration 0005:

  • 33 candidates promoted to active themes, 10 of them in a new group, 섹터 (equity sector views), so there are now 18 groups;
  • 229 merged: 147 into the new themes and 82 into 28 existing ones, each straight to its final theme;
  • 160 left as candidates. These are 22 in eight deferred proposals (five BlackRock-only themes and three single-house themes), 67 that failed the check, and 71 single-signal candidates the list never placed.

About 448 signals now resolve to an active theme, 357 of them eligible to count. No embedding call was needed: each promoted theme keeps its candidate's vector.

A subtle part: the 신규 badge. 신규 marks a theme as new. It needs two things:

  • a promotion date (promoted_at) in the run's week or the week before;
  • at least two reports in those weeks.

Stamp the 33 promotions with today's date, and the next runs would mark many of them 신규: a flood of "new" badges for topics the reports had been discussing for months. The migration therefore dates each promotion two weeks before the week of the theme's first report. That makes 신규 impossible for them, past or future, by construction. Had each promotion been dated in the week of its first report instead, 8 rows in the 26 weeks would have shown 신규. The themes are new to the dictionary, not to the reports.

2.5 Hiding the candidates

The backend now treats a candidate exactly like a retired theme. Its signals leave every surface:

  • list chips and the "+n more" count;
  • detail cards and bullet chips;
  • matched stocks;
  • the marks on a theme page.

A theme filter naming only candidates matches nothing. A candidate's theme page answers with its status and empty parts, and the frontend shows the signed "not found" screen. Why hide them like this, rather than show them with a "draft" badge? The aggregation writes no rows for candidates. A candidate's theme page would always show an empty trend, so hiding it is the only state that stays consistent everywhere.

One report shows the effect well: a 9/1 Bank of America Institute report. Before, it had 6 signal cards, most of them on candidates. Hiding alone left 2. Hiding plus the dictionary gave 3, because four restaurant candidates were aliased to 소비 지출 동향 (consumer spending). The dictionary also keeps hiding from emptying the screens:

  • with hiding alone, 3 reports in the 10/5 week (62 over 26 weeks) would lose every chip;
  • with the dictionary, 0 in the 10/5 week (19 over 26 weeks).

One gap was left open on purpose, and gets fixed in the update. A stance or horizon filter on its own can still match a report through a hidden candidate signal. On the untouched copy, 63 of the 198 reports matching stance=bull show no bull chip; after the dictionary, 18 do.

Excluding candidates by listing them in the URL would take about 14,600 characters for 422 ids, and the client library already warns at 8,000. The fix filters on the signal's own theme status instead. It was verified on the local copy (198 → 180 results, gap 18 → 0), and you chose to ship it in the update.

2.6 Dates are data: HSBC and Merrill

The weekly collector runs every Sunday at 09:37 KST and looks back 14 days. A report's date decides which week it counts in, and whether the collector keeps it at all. HSBC US lists its reports as PDF links with labels in three shapes:

  • a day label, such as "17 September 2026";
  • a month-only label, such as "October 2026";
  • no date at all, such as "HSBC Perspectives Q4 2026 – …".

The old code had a fault for each shape:

  1. Month-only labels were dated on the crawl day. The date parser (dateutil) fills any missing field from a default, and its default is today. On this machine, on 10/16, parse("October 2026") returns 2026-10-16. The September monthly, crawled on 9/13, is stored as 9/13. If the old code had found the October monthly on the 10/18 run, it would have dated it 10/18, in the following week. Collection never corrects a stored date, so that wrong week would have stuck.
  2. Labels with no date were dropped. With no date, the 14-day window check throws the item away, so HSBC's quarterly outlook was never collected.
  3. Listing lag. HSBC often lists a PDF days after its date. The 9/17 special was not on the listing on 9/28, and by 10/4 it was older than the 14-day cutoff. It was never collected.

The fix keeps a day label as it is. For the other two shapes it asks the server one cheap question: one HEAD request for the PDF's Last-Modified header.

  • Month-only label: use that header's date when it falls inside the labelled month, otherwise the 1st of the month. Never the crawl day.
  • Undated label: use the header, or drop the item as before.
  • The window: HSBC's is now at least 45 days, whatever the cron says.
  • Provenance: each date records where it came from, in metadata.published_at_source.

A failed HEAD never fails the run; it only selects the fallback. Seeing an old item again is harmless, because registration matches on the house and the canonical URL and keeps fields that are already filled.

Run live with the cron's 14-day window, the old code returned only the October monthly, dated on the crawl day. The fixed code returns four items:

  • the October monthly, dated 10/9;
  • the 17 September special;
  • Perspectives Q4, dated 10/13;
  • the September monthly.

Merrill's problem was patience, not dates. Its category pages took 4.4 to 13.5 seconds to answer, against a global timeout of 10 seconds with two attempts. Its link collection failed or partly failed in each of the last five runs. The fix gives Merrill's own requests 30 seconds and leaves every other house at 10.

At 30 s, a test run loaded 3 of the 5 category pages plus the CMO hub, which is where the weekly CMOs are. At 60 s all five loaded, so 60 s was suggested. 30 s was kept for three reasons:

  • the same pages had answered in 4–14 s earlier that night, and the slowness came after a 49-minute crawl from the same machine, which looks like throttling;
  • the two pages that still timed out hold mostly evergreen planning articles;
  • 60 s would double the worst case on a slow day.

If the weekly runs still show Merrill failures, the fix is one constant.

2.8 Would a smarter model help?

The team's quality bar for signal extraction asks that at least 18 of 20 sampled signals carry the right theme. The current model, gpt-5.6-terra, scored 16 of 20 in the earlier pilot. The phase re-ran the same 29 reports on two larger models:

ModelTheme right95 % Wilson intervalCost (cap)
gpt-5.6-terra (today)16 / 2058 – 92 %—
gpt-6.1-sol15 / 18 (11 reports gave no signals, leaving the sample 2 short)61 – 94 %$0.43 ($2.00)
gpt-5.6-sol15 / 2053 – 89 %$1.30 ($3.00)

Twenty samples are very few. Even a model that is truly right 90 % of the time clears 18 of 20 only about 68 % of the time. Its chance of 18, 19 or 20 successes in 20 tries is 0.677. The paired look was more telling:

  • both larger models fixed the same 8 of the 11 items terra still got wrong;
  • but they broke 11 and 7 of the 36 items terra had right.

A bigger model changed which errors were made, not how many. The phase recommends keeping terra. gpt-5.6-sol costs 2.33 times as much for no gain. gpt-6.1-sol is 23 % cheaper, but it loses recall on short reports. This test was the largest single cost of the phase, $1.73.

2.9 Cooling and conflicts: fine as they are

With rising explained, you asked the same of the other two lists: "research how we can increase cooling and conflict also. if it's not possible, fine as is." The answer was fine as is.

  • Cooling. At 10/5 only 6 themes reach the 0.5 floor at all. Every near-miss is either the HSBC gap (21 of 27) or a 3 → 0 drop of the kind chance produces about one time in five (2.3). Over 26 weeks, cooling fills all five slots in 3 weeks, and in none of the last 9. The only honest lever is the HSBC fix already made.
  • Conflicts. The near-misses are of two kinds:
    • opposite stances on different horizons (7);
    • bull or bear against neutral (9).
    The roadmap's "same theme and horizon" rule excludes both, rightly. The dictionary adds no conflicts. Neutral is 8.5 % of signals, mostly HSBC's genuinely neutral table cells, and not overused. The two-report filter has never removed a pair. Conflicts fill all three slots in 16 of 26 weeks and in 4 of the last 9. They fell after July because HSBC, J.P. Morgan and Jefferies took fewer bull or bear sides, not because of a rule.

The research found one optional change, called K1. Each house's side in a conflict would come from its latest report in the 8-week window, instead of its 8-week majority. At 10/5 that would add AI 자본지출 · 장기: HSBC US and Morgan Stanley bull, against Goldman Sachs and J.P. Morgan, whose newest reports turned bear on 9/23 and 10/1. That is the roadmap's own example of a split. Conflicts at 10/5 would go 1 → 2, and over 26 weeks 117 → 127 (20 added, 10 removed). It makes the list more current, not fuller. It changes a value 대표 approved, so it is not part of today's update.

3.2 The migration, 0005

schema/0005_theme_dictionary_supplement.sql is 544 lines, most of them its two lists. It runs in a single transaction with the same steps every time:

  1. load the lists into temporary tables;
  2. check that the database is in the state the file expects;
  3. change the themes;
  4. move the aliases;
  5. check the result;
  6. commit.

If any check fails, it raises an error and nothing is written.

Every UPDATE is filtered by the row's current state (AND t.status = 'candidate'), so a second run changes nothing. Here is the promotion, with the backdated promoted_at: the Monday of the first report's KST week, minus 14 days.

UPDATE public.research_themes t
   SET status          = 'active',
       theme_canonical = p.theme_canonical,
       theme_group     = p.theme_group,
       promoted_at     = f.promoted_at,
       updated_at      = now()
  FROM supplement_promote p
  JOIN (SELECT c.promoted_id,
               (date_trunc('week', min(d.published_at) AT TIME ZONE 'Asia/Seoul')
                - interval '14 days') AT TIME ZONE 'Asia/Seoul' AS promoted_at
          FROM ...   -- the theme's own signals plus those of candidates merged into it
         GROUP BY c.promoted_id) f ON f.promoted_id = p.theme_id
 WHERE t.theme_id = p.theme_id
   AND t.status = 'candidate';

The merge points each candidate straight at its final active theme, never at another merged theme. Its auto alias moves with it and becomes reviewed. That matters because the signal stage ignores an alias whose theme is merged; it does not follow merged_into. The aggregation does follow merged_into when it reads, so re-running past weeks picks up the change without rewriting a single signal. The final check refuses to commit unless three things hold:

  • every promoted theme is active, with a date and an embedding;
  • every merged theme points at an active one;
  • no alias still points at a merged theme.

A separate file, checks/0005_verify.sql, holds 31 read-only checks to run after the apply.

3.3 HSBC's dates and Merrill's timeout

The HSBC fix is one new method in HsbcUsSource (collection/adapters.py), plus two helpers. It reads like the rules in 2.6:

def _publication_date(
    self, http_client: HttpClient, url: str, label: str,
) -> tuple[datetime | None, str | None]:
    date_text = self._extract_date_text(label)
    if date_text and date_text[0].isdigit():  # "17 September 2026", not "September 2026"
        published_at = parse_datetime(date_text)
        if published_at is not None:
            return published_at, "label_day"
    last_modified = self._last_modified(http_client, url)
    month_span = self._month_span(date_text) if date_text else None
    if month_span is None:
        return (last_modified, "last_modified") if last_modified else (None, None)
    start, end = month_span
    if last_modified is not None and start <= last_modified < end:
        return last_modified, "last_modified"
    return start, "label_month_start"

The leading-digit test is what keeps dateutil away from month-only labels, so it never gets the chance to fill in today. _last_modified makes one HEAD request and returns None on any failure. That way a slow or broken server only selects the fallback and never changes the run's exit code. The window is one override:

min_window_days = 45

def resolve_window_days(self) -> int:
    return max(super().resolve_window_days(), self.min_window_days)

3.4 The backend: one set of hidden ids

The backend change in app/research/dashboard/reads.py works because the retired-theme rule already sat in one place. The dictionary class gains a candidate set defined exactly like the retired one, by the end of a theme's merge chain, plus their union:

def candidate_ids(self) -> Set[str]:
    if self._candidate is None:
        self._candidate = {
            theme_id
            for theme_id, target in self._resolved.items()
            if self._rows[target].get("status") == "candidate"
        }
    return set(self._candidate)

def hidden_ids(self) -> Set[str]:
    """Ids whose signals the dashboard never shows: `retired_ids()` plus `candidate_ids()`."""
    return self.retired_ids() | self.candidate_ids()

The one read that decides which signals are visible swaps retired for hidden. Because every screen draws from that read, the chips, cards, bullet chips, matched stocks and theme-page marks all change together:

hidden = themes.hidden_ids()

Two smaller changes complete it:

  • the theme page sends hidden themes to _hidden_theme_page (renamed from _retired_theme_page), which answers 200 with the real status and empty parts, so links in mails never break.

4. Best practices & next steps

How does this work compare with common practice outside this codebase? Each claim below links a page that was opened for this explainer.

Where it lines up

  • All-or-nothing, self-checking migrations. PostgreSQL's documentation says RAISE EXCEPTION "raises an error (which normally aborts the current transaction)" (PostgreSQL docs: Errors and Messages — postgresql.org). 0005 puts every change between BEGIN and COMMIT and guards it with such checks. A failed guard leaves production exactly as it was, and a second run is a no-op. That is what makes it safe for you to apply by hand.
  • A per-request timeout for one slow host. HTTPX lets you "set timeouts for an individual request", and a timeout set on a client is used "as the default" (HTTPX docs: Timeouts — python-httpx.org). Merrill gets 30 s on its own calls. Every other house still fails fast at 10 s.
  • An auto margin instead of measuring. MDN explains that margin-left: auto "consumes all the space it can in its axis", pushing an item away from its neighbours (MDN: Aligning items in a flex container — developer.mozilla.org). On one line it keeps the count at the right edge, as before. Once flex-wrap moves the count to its own line, the same margin right-aligns it there, with no JavaScript.
  • Not trusting a parser's defaults. dateutil's documentation says missing elements "are taken from the datetime.datetime object passed to the parameter default" (dateutil docs: parser — dateutil.readthedocs.io). When no default is passed, the source sets it to datetime.datetime.now().replace(hour=0, minute=0, …) (dateutil source: parser.parse — raw.githubusercontent.com). That one line is the whole HSBC month-label bug. The fix decides by the label's shape first, and lets the parser see only labels that carry a day.

Where it deliberately departs

  • A file date standing in for a publication date. MDN describes Last-Modified as the time "when the origin server believes the resource was last modified", and as "less accurate than an ETag" (MDN: Last-Modified — developer.mozilla.org). It is a file's date, not a publication date: a re-upload moves it. The fix accepts it only inside the month the label names, and otherwise falls back to the 1st. That is a reasonable trade here:
    • the label already bounds the month;
    • the collector may not download report content (the capture stage owns that);
    • one HEAD request per PDF is cheap.
  • Deciding a model choice on 20 samples. Statisticians prefer the Wilson score interval for small samples because it "can be safely employed with small samples and skewed observations" (Wikipedia: Binomial proportion confidence interval — en.wikipedia.org). Terra's 16/20 is compatible with a true rate from about 58 % to 92 %. The phase kept terra because the evidence could not separate the models and terra costs less, not because the models were shown to be equal.

Next steps

  1. Read the new date field. Every HSBC date now records its source: label_day, last_modified or label_month_start. A rising share of label_month_start in the weekly runs would be the first sign that HSBC changed its labels or re-uploads its files.
  2. Count what houses publish, not only what we collect. The thin week was explained only by counting each house's output by hand (about 17 in scope, 8 collected). A per-house published-versus-collected count in the weekly report would surface the next silent gap within a week.
  3. Use larger labelled samples before model decisions. At 20 samples, a model that is truly right 90 % of the time fails an 18-of-20 bar about one time in three. A few times more labelled signals would narrow the intervals enough to tell a real gain from luck (Wikipedia: Binomial proportion confidence interval — en.wikipedia.org).

1. Before the fix, why would HSBC's October monthly have landed in the wrong week?

dateutil's default for missing fields is today at midnight. "October 2026" parsed on 10/16 gives 10/16; found on the 10/18 run, the monthly would have been dated 10/18. Collection never corrects a stored date.

2. Migration 0005 dates each promotion two weeks before the week of the theme's first report. What is that for?

The aggregation follows merges whatever the date. The backdate only keeps 신규 from lighting up for 33 themes the reports had been discussing for months.

3. In the rehearsed catch-up, rising filled to five but 일본주식 강세 stayed in cooling. Why?

The October monthly was registered and extracted. It just says nothing readable about Japanese equities, so the drop stands, now from houses that are publishing, which the rules allow.

4. Why not lower the cooling floor to 0.25 to fill the cooling list?

A theme with 3 votes in 8 weeks goes silent for 4 weeks with probability about e^−1.5 ≈ 0.22 even if nothing changed. With about 49 such themes, roughly 10 would "cool" each week by luck.

5. Terra scored 16/20 on theme accuracy and gpt-5.6-sol scored 15/20. What does that show?

With 20 samples, the 95 % Wilson intervals are about 35 points wide. Both larger models fixed some of terra's errors and broke others, so terra stays, for its lower cost.
