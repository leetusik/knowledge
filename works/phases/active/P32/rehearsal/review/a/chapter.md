Rome is the rare state whose story runs for more than two thousand years: from a village on a hill to a Mediterranean empire, and then, in the East, to a medieval city-state that fell to cannon fire in 1453. This chapter walks that whole arc in order, so that by the end you can name the turning points and say why each one mattered.

## The shape of the story {#shape}

> [!IMPORTANT] The one idea to hold on to
> Rome did not "fall" once. It changed form again and again: **kings, then a Republic, then emperors, then two halves**. The western half ended in 476; the eastern half, which called itself Roman throughout, lasted until 1453.

## Founding: legend and evidence {#founding}

**Rome's famous birthday is a legend, not a recorded fact.** According to tradition, the brothers Romulus and Remus founded the city on 21 April 753 BCE; the same source notes that the date's historicity is uncertain, yet Rome still celebrates it as its birthday ([World History Encyclopedia, "Rome"](#src-rome)). The story says the city was then ruled by seven kings, from Romulus to Tarquin, and that the Etruscans to the north provided a model for trade and urban luxury, though historians debate how big their role was ([same source](#src-rome)).

Archaeology gives a murkier but not empty picture. In 2005 the archaeologist Andrea Carandini reported a palace near the Sanctuary of Vesta and a wall he dated to roughly 775-750 BCE, and argued that the evidence supports a real Romulus; many colleagues dispute that reading, and most classical scholars treat Romulus and Remus as legendary ([NBC News report](#src-nbc)). The safe summary: a settlement on the Palatine Hill existed around the time of the legend, and the founder-hero is a story.

## From kings to a Republic {#republic}

According to the tradition, the last king, Tarquin the Proud, was overthrown in 510 BCE; the source's own data also gives 509 BCE, and either date is commonly used for the start of the Republic ([World History Encyclopedia, "Roman Republic"](#src-republic)). A republic here means a state run by elected officials and a Senate rather than a king.

The Republic's great external story is its war with the North African city of Carthage. The three Punic Wars ran from 264 to 146 BCE, and the last (149-146 BCE) ended with Rome destroying the city ([same source](#src-republic)). Victory made Rome the dominant power around the Mediterranean.

Success then strained the system. In 133 BCE the tribune Tiberius Gracchus pushed land reforms; he and his brother failed to make the changes needed. Then came generals who used armies against each other: the first civil war between Marius and Sulla in 88-87 BCE, with Sulla marching on Rome, and Sulla's peaceful retirement in 79 BCE ([same source](#src-republic)).

## The Republic breaks, Augustus builds an empire {#augustus}

The last act is quick. On 10 January 49 BCE Julius Caesar crossed the Rubicon river with his 13th legion, starting a civil war; he was assassinated by senators on the Ides of March, 44 BCE, because they feared he was becoming too powerful; and on 2 September 31 BCE Octavian defeated Mark Antony and Cleopatra at Actium ([same source](#src-republic)).

Octavian, known as Augustus, ruled from 27 BCE until his death in 14 CE ([World History Encyclopedia, "Roman Empire"](#src-empire)). How he described this in his own words is revealing. In his *Res Gestae*, a first-person record of his deeds, he writes: "I restored liberty to the republic, which had been oppressed by the tyranny of a faction," and "I transferred the republic from my own control to the will of the Senate and the Roman people" (§1 and §34, [Livius.org translation](#src-resgestae)). He also lists thirteen consulships and says "Egypt I added to the empire of the Roman people" ([same source](#src-resgestae)).

> [!NOTE]- Why read Augustus's own account with care
> This is a self-portrait by the man in power. It tells you how he wanted the change to look: a restored republic, not a monarchy. It is evidence of his framing, not a neutral verdict.

## The Roman Peace {#peace}

The first two centuries of the empire are called the *Pax Romana*, the "Roman Peace." One reference work says it lasted over 200 years, beginning with Augustus, and that the empire stayed relatively free of civil conflict during it ([World History Encyclopedia, "Pax Romana"](#src-pax)). The same source adds the other side: wars on the frontiers continued, Hadrian called an end to expansion and fixed the northern borders, and a Briton named Calgacus is quoted as saying of the Romans, "they create desolation and call it peace" ([same source](#src-pax)).

At its height around 117 CE the empire was the most extensive political structure in the western world, and in 212 CE Caracalla's edict granted Roman citizenship to all free inhabitants ([World History Encyclopedia, "Roman Empire"](#src-empire)).

## The third-century crisis {#crisis}

**Peace gave way to half a century of chaos.** The Crisis of the Third Century (235-284) saw over 20 emperors rise and fall in almost fifty years. In 260 a governor, Postumus, broke away and ruled Gaul, Germania, Hispania and Britannia; around 270 Queen Zenobia of Palmyra formed her own empire in the east. Emperor Aurelian (270-275) reunited the empire by defeating both ([World History Encyclopedia, "Crisis of the Third Century"](#src-crisis)).

Diocletian (284-305) is credited with ending the crisis. His key move was the tetrarchy, "rule of four," dividing the work of governing the huge empire, with the system dated to 293 ([same source](#src-crisis)). Notice the pattern: the empire survived by splitting the load of rule.

## Constantine and the new capital {#constantine}

Constantine defeated his rival Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge in 312. Soon after, he issued the Edict of Milan, a toleration of all religions (313); after beating Licinius he was sole emperor in 324; he summoned church leaders to the Council of Nicaea in 325; and in 330 he dedicated Constantinople, built on the site of the older city of Byzantium. He died in 337 ([World History Encyclopedia, "Constantine the Great"](#src-constantine)).

> [!IMPORTANT] Why Constantinople matters
> It gave the empire a second centre of gravity in the east. Later, when the west collapsed, the east already had a capital, an administration and an emperor.

## The West ends in 476 {#west}

On 4 September 476 CE, two days after the Battle of Ravenna, the young emperor Romulus Augustulus was deposed and, in one account, "the Roman Empire in the west was finished"; Odoacer declared himself king ([World History Encyclopedia, "Odoacer"](#src-odoacer)). Notice the neat irony: the last western emperor shares his name with the legendary founder, Romulus.

> [!NOTE]- Was 476 really an ending?
> A date like 476 is a convenient marker, not a single event that people at the time experienced as "the fall of Rome." This chapter's sources describe the deposition itself, not a debate over how sudden the decline was, so treat that larger question as open here.

## The eastern empire: Justinian to the walls {#east}

The eastern half kept the name Roman. Under Justinian I (527-565) and his general Belisarius, it won back territories in North Africa, Spain and Italy, and rebuilt the Hagia Sophia church in 532-537 ([World History Encyclopedia, "Byzantine Empire"](#src-byz)). Then came losses: the Islamic conquests of the 7th and 8th centuries took the Levant (Jerusalem fell in 637), though Constantinople withstood Arab sieges in 674-678 and 717-718, and the dispute over religious images, iconoclasm, ran in two phases, 726-787 and 814-843 ([same source](#src-byz)).

In 1204 the armies of the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople; the empire survived in exile until its restoration in 1261 ([same source](#src-byz)).

> [!DEFINITION] "Byzantine" is a later label
> The term was coined by 16th-century historians, after Byzantium, the city's old name. The people themselves considered themselves Romans, called the emperor "Emperor of the Romans," and named their capital "New Rome." Greek was the most common language, so the state was culturally more Greek than Roman ([same source](#src-byz)).

## 1453: the fall of Constantinople {#fall}

The last act was a siege. The Ottoman army assembled at the walls on 2 April 1453 and bombardment began on 6 April. The final assault came at dawn on Tuesday 29 May 1453, after about six weeks ([World History Encyclopedia, "1453"](#src-1453)). An eyewitness put the attackers at 200,000, but modern historians estimate 60,000 to 80,000; the defenders numbered fewer than 5,000 ([same source](#src-1453)).

The Theodosian Walls, completed in 439, ran some 6.5 kilometres, with an inner wall almost 5 metres thick and 96 projecting towers. Against them the Ottomans used huge cannon built by a Hungarian engineer called Urban, whom the emperor had been unable to afford; the biggest could fire a 500-kilogram ball over 1.5 km, but only seven times a day ([same source](#src-1453)).

Constantine XI, who reigned from 1449, was killed in the final assault. Constantinople became the Ottoman capital and Hagia Sophia a mosque; the last Byzantine remnants fell at Mistra in 1460 and Trebizond in 1461 ([same source](#src-1453)).

> [!NOTE]- Sources disagree on small numbers
> The start of the Republic is given as 510 or 509 BCE, and different works count the siege as 53, 55 or "about six weeks." Treat precise day counts and such dates as approximate.

## What to take away {#takeaway}

- **Rome is a chain of forms**: monarchy (legend), Republic, empire, then two halves.
- **Three hinges**: the civil wars of the first century BCE, the third-century crisis, and Constantine's new capital.
- **Two endings**: 476 for the West, 1453 for the East.
